Group 1 — Triangle Centres from Given Data G1Ex1 Q2, Q3, Q14 · Ex2 Q1, Q8, Q11 · Ex3 Q7, Q41, Q96

1Orthocentre: two altitudes. With two vertices and \(H\) known, write \(AH\perp BC\) and \(BH\perp AC\) as dot products (Ex1 Q3: \(C=(33,26)\)).
2Circumcentre: on the perpendicular bisectors; use the side whose endpoints are known (Ex3 Q41: bisector of \(PQ\) is \(y=x\), giving \((-2,-2)\)).
3Euler line: \(G=\tfrac{2O+H}3\); \(O,G,H\) are collinear with \(OG:GH=1:2\) (Ex3 Q96: \(G=(0,0)\)).
4Incentre of a right triangle with legs \(p,q\): \((r,r)\), \(r=\tfrac{p+q-\text{hyp}}2\) (Ex3 Q1: \(2-\sqrt2\)). Midpoints \(M_1,M_2,M_3\) give vertices \(A=M_1+M_3-M_2\).
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. List what is given: vertices, a centre, side lines.
  2. Pick the property that uses it: altitude ⊥ side, point on a perpendicular bisector, or \(G\) dividing the median 2:1.
  3. Turn each property into one linear equation; solve.
  4. Check with a second property (equal distance, or another altitude).
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Missing vertex from \(H\): two perpendicularity equations (use dot products if a side is vertical).
  • Circumcentre: intersect two perpendicular bisectors, or solve \(|OA|=|OB|=|OC|\).
  • Area-with-parameter (Ex3 Q7): \(\tfrac12|\det|=28\) first, keep only the allowed (integer) value, then the altitudes.
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Right triangle: circumcentre = midpoint of the hypotenuse; orthocentre = the right-angle vertex.
  • If two of \(O,G,H\) are given, the third follows from the Euler line.
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Using \(m_1=m_2\) instead of \(m_1m_2=-1\) → write the perpendicularity condition explicitly.
  • Using the wrong midpoint → name the side before taking its midpoint.
  • Using \((O+H)/2\) for \(G\) → remember \(G=(2O+H)/3\).

Group 2 — Bisectors, Reflections & Shortest Path G2Ex1 Q19 · Ex2 Q8, Q9, Q14 · Ex3 Q45, Q51, Q94

5Image of \((x_1,y_1)\) in \(ax+by+c=0\): \(\tfrac{x'-x_1}a=\tfrac{y'-y_1}b=\tfrac{-2(ax_1+by_1+c)}{a^2+b^2}\).
6Shortest path via a mirror: reflect one point, join to the other (Ex3 Q51: \(R=(-1,0)\), answer 1250). A light ray: reflect the source.
7Angle bisectors: \(\tfrac{a_1x+b_1y+c_1}{\sqrt{a_1^2+b_1^2}}=\pm\tfrac{a_2x+b_2y+c_2}{\sqrt{a_2^2+b_2^2}}\); pick the sign using a given point (Ex3 Q45). Perpendicular bisectors of \(AB\), \(AC\) make \(B\), \(C\) images of \(A\) (Ex2 Q8).
8Distance measured parallel to a line: move along \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) of that line and substitute (Ex3 Q94: \(4+6\sqrt3\)).
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. Is it a reflection (mirror, light ray, shortest path), a bisector (equal distances) or a measured-along-a-direction distance?
  2. Reflection: reflect one point and join. Bisector: use the ± distance equation.
  3. For an angle at a vertex, decide interior vs exterior by the side of a given point.
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Median plus bisector (Ex3 Q45): the median fixes \(C\); then take the angle between \(CA\) and the bisector — it is \(\tfrac\theta2\).
  • Perpendicular bisectors (Ex2 Q8): \(B\) and \(C\) are the images of \(A\) in the two bisectors; the line \(BC\) joins them.
  • Parallel distance: parametrise \((x_0+t\cos\theta,y_0+t\sin\theta)\), substitute, take \(|t|\).
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Check a reflection by the midpoint lying on the mirror.
  • A reflected ray is the line through the image and the end point.
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Reflecting both points → reflect only one.
  • Using the angle with the bisector as the whole angle → it is half.
  • Measuring perpendicular distance when \"parallel to\" is asked.

Group 3 — Parallelograms, Rhombus & Rectangles G3Ex1 Q10, Q15 · Ex2 Q7 · Ex3 Q15, Q99

9Diagonals bisect each other: \(A+C=B+D\) (Ex3 Q99: \(C=(-3,-11),D=(-6,-12)\), \(|\text{sum}|=32\)).
10Two side lines + diagonal point \(O\): the sides meet at \(V\); opposite vertex \(=2O-V\); another vertex \(P\) on one line has \(2O-P\) on the other (Ex3 Q15: \((3,6)\)).
11Rectangle: diagonals equal and bisect; other vertices lie on the line at the half-diagonal distance from the midpoint (Ex1 Q15: \(c=-4\), \((4,4),(2,0)\)).
12Rhombus: diagonals are perpendicular and bisect the angles between the side lines.
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. Name the shape's property: diagonals bisect (parallelogram), equal (rectangle), perpendicular (rhombus).
  2. Write it as a vector equation \(A+C=B+D\).
  3. Use the given lines to say which vertices lie on them.
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Parallelogram from two side lines and the diagonal point: vertex \(V\) at the meeting of the sides, opposite \(2O-V\), then the pair \(P,\,2O-P\) on the two lines.
  • Rectangle: midpoint on the line; move along it by the half-diagonal.
  • Vertices with unknown pairs: substitute \(C\) and \(D\) in their lines and solve the linear system.
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Sum of all four coordinates is twice the diagonal midpoint's coordinates' sum — a quick check.
  • Test the candidate vertex: both it and its opposite must lie on the right side lines.
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Pairing the vertices wrongly in \(A+C=B+D\).
  • Choosing a vertex that fits one line but whose opposite fits neither.
  • Using the wrong half-diagonal length.

Group 4 — Area, Regions & Ratios G4Ex1 Q23 · Ex3 Q32, Q48, Q49, Q97

13Area = ½|det|. \"Area \(=k\)\" gives two parallel lines at distance \(2k/\text{base}\) (Ex3 Q48: least distance \(8/\sqrt5\)).
14Equal areas \(APB=BPC=CPA\) ⇒ \(P\) is the centroid (Ex3 Q32: \(PQ=5\)).
15Region containing a point: evaluate each line at that point to fix the sign, then apply it to the moving point (Ex3 Q97: \((-3,0)\cup(\tfrac13,1)\)).
16\(|\text{expr}|=c\) gives two cases; add or combine both (Ex3 Q49: sum \(=\tfrac{-2b^2}{b+1}\)).
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. Is it an area, an equal area or a region question?
  2. Area given: two parallel lines. Equal areas: centroid. Region: signs at a test point.
  3. Solve, then test the endpoints/intervals.
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Area \(=\tfrac12|x_1(y_2-y_3)+x_2(y_3-y_1)+x_3(y_1-y_2)|\); with a parameter, keep ±.
  • Distance between the parallel lines of area \(k\): \(\tfrac{2k}{\text{base}}\).
  • Inequalities for a moving point \((a^2,a+1)\): substitute into each line and solve both quadratic inequalities.
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Sketch the two lines and shade the region.
  • Take the nearer line when asked for the least distance.
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Dropping the ½ in the area formula.
  • Keeping only one sign of ±.
  • Forgetting to intersect two inequality ranges.

Group 5 — Locus & Parameter Conditions G5Ex1 Q9 · Ex2 Q15, Q17, Q18, Q20 · Ex3 Q31, Q36, Q57, Q100

17Locus: let the point be \((h,k)\), write the relation, eliminate the parameter, then replace \((h,k)\) by \((x,y)\).
18Hidden fixed point: \(\tfrac1a+\tfrac1b=\tfrac12\) means \(\tfrac xa+\tfrac yb=1\) passes through \((2,2)\) (Ex3 Q36).
19\"Do not form a triangle\": two lines parallel or all three concurrent; list every case (Ex3 Q100: \(\alpha=\pm4,\tfrac45\); \(p=32.64\)).
20Sum of squared distances constant ⇒ a circle; use its axis chords (Ex3 Q57: \(\tfrac12\cdot3\cdot\sqrt{17}\)).
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. What is moving, and what is fixed? Name the parameter.
  2. Write the condition as an equation, solve for the parameter, substitute.
  3. For \"no triangle\" list all failure cases: parallel pairs, concurrency.
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Locus of a point (h,k): eliminate the parameter, then replace (h,k).
  • Hidden fixed point in a family of lines: rewrite the condition in intercept form.
  • Circle from \(PA^2+PB^2=k\): expand, read off intercepts on the axes.
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Test a locus with a special position of the moving line.
  • Count the parameter values: parallel (2 of them), concurrent (1).
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Treating \"no triangle\" as parallel only.
  • Forgetting a second root from \(|\cdot|=c\).
  • Using the wrong formula for the area of the quadrilateral (use ½ d₁ d₂ for perpendicular diagonals).

Group 6 — Hidden Forms & Composite Problems G6Ex3 Q26, Q83, Q86, Q98

21A trig expression that \"is a line\": simplify with product-to-sum first; it may be a constant (Ex3 Q26: \(y=-\sin^21\)).
22Isosceles with \(120^\circ\): sides \(c,c,c\sqrt3\); place the vertices by rotating about the apex (Ex3 Q98: answer 36).
233-D quadrilateral area: \(\tfrac12|\overrightarrow{AC}\times\overrightarrow{BD}|\) (Ex3 Q83: \(8\sqrt{38}\)).
24Centroid with parameters: write each vertex as the meeting point of two side lines, average, then solve (Ex3 Q86: \(a=3,p=11\), \(BC^2=122\)).
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. Is a piece of the problem secretly simple? (a constant, a 120° triangle, a 3-D vector area.)
  2. Simplify first, then place the figure in coordinates.
  3. Solve for parameters, reject degenerate cases.
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Product-to-sum: \(\sin x\sin(x+2)=\tfrac12[\cos2-\cos(2x+2)]\) turns a \"curve\" into a line.
  • Rotate a vector by \(120^\circ\) about the apex to get the third vertex.
  • Centroid conditions give two equations per parameter; solve all together.
💡 Tips & tricks
  • For \((BC)^2\) after finding vertices, just use the distance formula.
  • Reject any solution that collapses two vertices.
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Using \(BC^2=2c^2\) (right-angle) instead of \(3c^2\) for a \(120^\circ\) apex.
  • Forgetting the ½ in the vector-area formula.
  • Keeping a degenerate root (like \(a=0\)).
