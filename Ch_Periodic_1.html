1 · Development of the Periodic Table

The big idea. With 118 elements, nobody can study each one separately, so chemists looked for a pattern. Each attempt fixed a problem of the one before. The big jump was Moseley: elements should be ordered by atomic number Z, not atomic mass. That gave the modern periodic law: the properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic numbers.

Who did what

ScientistIdeaWhy it fell short
Döbereiner (triads)Groups of three similar elements; the middle atomic mass is the mean of the other two (Li 7, Na 23, K 39; 23 = (7 + 39)/2). Fe, Co, Ni have nearly equal masses.Worked for only a few elements
Newlands (law of octaves)First to link chemical properties with atomic masses. The 8th element from any element resembles it (Li … Na … K).Worked for lighter elements only, failed for heavier ones
Lothar MeyerPlotted atomic volume (atomic mass ÷ density of the solid) against atomic mass. Atomic volume is a periodic function of atomic mass.Lacked practical use
MendeleevProperties are periodic functions of atomic masses. 9 vertical groups (I–VIII and zero) and 7 periods; blank spaces left for undiscovered elements.See the demerits below
Moseley (long form)Characteristic X-ray frequency: √ν = a(Z − b). Atomic number is more fundamental than atomic mass.Basis of the modern table

Memory hooks
  • D-N-M-M: Döbereiner (triads), Newlands (octaves), Meyer (volume curve), Mendeleev (mass table), then Moseley (atomic number).
  • Meyer's curve: alkali metals on the crests, transition metals in the troughs.
  • Moseley fixes Mendeleev. Every mass-order violation (Ar/K, Te/I) disappears when you order by Z.
  • Gallium and germanium are the two famous predictions: eka-aluminium and eka-silicon.
Traps and exceptions
  • Mendeleev used atomic mass; the modern table uses atomic number. Do not mix up the two laws.
  • Ar before K in the modern table because Z(Ar) = 18 < Z(K) = 19, even though Ar is heavier.
  • Isotopes share one position in the modern table because they have the same Z.
  • Newlands was the first to relate chemical properties to atomic masses, but his octaves broke down after calcium.
  • Meyer's curve is a physical property (atomic volume), while Mendeleev's table used both physical and chemical properties.
  • The long form is also called "Bohr's table" because it follows Bohr's electronic-configuration scheme, with contributions from Ramsey, Werner, Bury and Moseley.
  • Source typo: the PDF lists gallium's melting point as 30.2 in a "K" column. Gallium melts at about 30 °C (303 K).
Worked example: why is argon placed before potassium?

Ar has Z = 18 and K has Z = 19. The modern law orders elements by atomic number, so Ar comes first even though its atomic mass (39.94) is greater than K's (39.1).

2 · The Modern Table: Periods, Groups & Blocks

The big idea. The table is a map of electron configurations. The period is the highest principal quantum number n. The block is the subshell receiving the last (differentiating) electron. The group comes from counting valence electrons. If you can write the configuration, you can place any element.

Memory hooks
  • Period = n. Block = last subshell. Group = count electrons.
  • p-block group = 10 + valence electrons. Halogens ns²np⁵ → 17. Noble gases ns²np⁶ → 18. Group 14 is ns²np².
  • d-block group = (n−1)d + ns. Fe 3d⁶4s² → 8. Zn 3d¹⁰4s² → 12. Cu 3d¹⁰4s¹ → 11.
  • Period lengths: 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32.
  • Diagonal pairs: Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si (from the top-left, one step right and down).
Traps and exceptions
  • Third period has 8 elements, not 18. 4s is filled before 3d, so after 3p the next element goes into 4s.
  • Zn, Cd, Hg and Cn are d-block but not transition elements. A transition element has partly filled d orbitals in the neutral atom or in a stable ion; Zn²⁺ is 3d¹⁰.
  • Helium is s-block by configuration (1s²) but sits in group 18 with the noble gases.
  • Hydrogen is placed separately at the top. It resembles alkali metals (one s electron) and halogens (needs one electron).
  • All f-block elements belong to group 3. They are placed at the bottom only to avoid stretching the table.
  • Palladium is 4d¹⁰5s⁰, the exception to (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ns¹⁻².
  • Only 14 lanthanides and 14 actinides (Ce–Lu, Th–Lr), since the 4f and 5f subshells hold 14 electrons.
  • Atomic number of the elements above and below Ag (47): 29 (Cu) and 79 (Au).
Worked example: Z = 112 (period, block, group)

Configuration: [Rn] 5f¹⁴ 6d¹⁰ 7s². Last electron enters d, so d-block. Group = 10 + 2 = 12. Valence shell n = 7, so period 7.

Worked example: group of 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s²

Last electron enters 3d: d-block. Group = 6 + 2 = 8 (this is iron, period 4).

3 · Effective Nuclear Charge & Shielding

The big idea. An outer electron is pulled by the nucleus (charge +Z) but pushed away by the inner electrons. The inner electrons act as a screen. The net pull it actually feels is the effective nuclear charge, Zeff. Almost every periodic trend (size, IE, EA, EN) comes from how Zeff and the number of shells change.

Formulas to know cold

Effective nuclear charge
Zeff = Z − σ
σ = screening (shielding or Slater) constant
Screening power by subshell
s > p > d > f
for the same shell n; s electrons are closest to the nucleus
Radius and Zeff
r ∝ 1/Zeff
for the same number of shells
Proton-to-electron ratio
Z / (number of electrons)
F: 9/9 = 1 · F⁻: 9/10 = 0.9

Trends

DirectionWhat happens
Across a period, left to rightZ rises by 1, shielding barely changes, so Zeff increases; n is constant, so radius falls
Down a groupZ rises by more than 1 but extra inner shells screen it, so Zeff stays almost constant; n rises, so radius rises
Isoelectronic speciesSame screening, so Zeff increases with Z
Atom to cationFewer electrons, same Z: Zeff increases
Atom to anionMore electrons, same Z: Zeff decreases
Memory hooks
  • Inner electrons are bodyguards. The more of them, and the closer they are (s > p > d > f), the less pull the valence electron feels.
  • Zeff = Z − σ: never more than Z, never below the charge of the "core".
  • One electron only means no screening (H, He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺): σ = 0, so Zeff = Z.
Traps and exceptions
  • Zeff is not the same for every electron in an atom; it depends on the electron's distance from the nucleus.
  • d and f electrons shield poorly (their diffuse shapes leave the outer electrons exposed). This causes irregularities: the lanthanide contraction, the small size of Ga, and the near-equal sizes of 4d and 5d elements.
  • For isoelectronic species, Zeff increases with Z, not decreases.
  • Screening is not observed in one-electron ions such as He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺.
  • A cation of the same element always has a higher Zeff than its atom: Sn⁴⁺ has more Zeff than Sn.
  • Down a group Zeff is "almost" constant but the shells increase, and size wins.
Worked example: why is F⁻ bigger than F?

F has 9 protons and 9 electrons (ratio 9/9 = 1). F⁻ has 9 protons and 10 electrons (ratio 9/10 = 0.9). The nucleus holds the electrons less tightly and electron repulsion rises, so F⁻ is larger.

4 · Oxidation States & the Inert Pair Effect

The big idea. The oxidation state (O.S.) is the imaginary charge an atom would carry if every bonding pair were handed to the more electronegative atom. It is bookkeeping, not the real charge. The inert pair effect explains why heavy p-block elements prefer an O.S. two units below their group maximum.

Rules (in order of priority)

RuleOxidation state
Sum of all O.S. in a species= charge on the species
Atoms in an element (P₄, O₂, graphite, C₆₀)0
Monoatomic ion= its charge
F; alkali metals; alkaline earth metals; Al and Ga−1; +1; +2; +3, no exceptions (highest priority)
H+1, except −1 in hydrides of the metals above (NaH, CaH₂)
O−2, except −1 in peroxides (O₂²⁻); +1 or +2 with fluorine (O₂F₂, OF₂)
Nmostly −3 (NH₃, NH₄⁺, CN⁻); +3 in NF₃, +5 in NO₃⁻ (forced by F and O)
Cl, Br, Iusually −1; positive when bonded to more electronegative atoms (ICl: I is +1; BrCl₃: Br +3; Cl₂O: Cl +1)
S−2 in sulphides; can reach +6 (the group number)
Metalsusually positive. Gold is sometimes −1.

Higher-priority atoms are fixed first; the "flexible" atom takes whatever is left. In BaO₂, Ba must be +2, so each O is −1 (peroxide).

Memory hooks
  • F is always −1. Alkali +1, alkaline earth +2, Al/Ga +3. Fix these first, then O, then H, then solve for the unknown.
  • Heavy means low. In groups 13–15 the heaviest element prefers the oxidation state two below the group maximum: Tl⁺, Pb²⁺, Bi³⁺.
  • Strong oxidising agents: Pb⁴⁺ and Bi⁵⁺ (they want to drop to Pb²⁺ and Bi³⁺).
  • Maximum O.S. of a main-group element = its group number (S +6, Cl +7). Mn reaches +7 using all (n−1)d and ns electrons.
Traps and exceptions
  • O.S. is not the real charge. In covalent molecules it is an assumption of fully ionic bonds.
  • Average O.S.: in NH₄NO₂ nitrogen is −3 (in NH₄⁺) and +3 (in NO₂⁻), average 0. Always find each separately when the atoms differ.
  • Fe₃O₄ has Fe at +8/3 on average (really two Fe³⁺ and one Fe²⁺). Non-stoichiometric compounds always have a metal in at least two oxidation states.
  • Fluorine never shows a positive state, and O is positive only with F.
  • Gold can be −1 (an exception to "metals are never negative").
  • Last-member exceptions: Pb⁴⁺ and Bi⁵⁺ exist only with F or O, not with I or Br.
  • The inert pair is the ns² pair (6s² in Tl, Pb, Bi), not the 6p electrons.
Worked example: oxidation state of N in NO₃⁻

O is −2 (3 atoms = −6). Charge = −1, so N + (−6) = −1, giving N = +5.

Worked example: O.S. of Br in BrCl₃

Cl must be −1 (3 atoms = −3). The molecule is neutral, so Br = +3.

5 · Atomic & Ionic Radii

The big idea. An atom has no sharp edge, so "radius" means different things depending on how the atom is bonded. Size is decided by a tug-of-war: more shells make an atom bigger, higher Zeff makes it smaller. For ions, what matters is whether electrons have been removed or added.

Types of atomic radius

TypeDefinitionNotes
CovalentHalf the distance between two singly bonded like atoms: d(A–A) = 2r_ACl₂: 1.98 Å, so r = 0.99 Å. For unlike atoms with similar EN: d(A–B) = r_A + r_B. If the EN difference is large the actual bond is shorter than r_A + r_B.
IonicRadius of the ion in an ionic crystalCation < atom < anion
MetallicHalf the distance between adjacent nuclei in a metallic crystalMetallic radius > covalent radius; larger metallic radius means looser packing and weaker metallic bond
van der WaalsHalf the distance between nuclei of adjacent, non-bonded atoms in the solidNoble gases have only this; in non-metals both exist

Order: van der Waals > metallic > covalent. Examples: F covalent 72 pm; Ne (van der Waals) 160 pm. Halogen X–X bond lengths: F₂ 143, Cl₂ 199, Br₂ 228, I₂ 266 pm.

Ions and size

Cation
Smaller than its atom: fewer electrons, same Z, so Zeff rises; also the outer shell is often lost. More positive charge = smaller: Fe > Fe²⁺ > Fe³⁺; Pb²⁺ > Pb⁴⁺; Mn > Mn²⁺ > … > Mn⁷⁺.
Anion
Larger than its atom: more electrons than protons, so Zeff falls and repulsion rises. More negative = larger: O < O⁻ < O²⁻.
Isoelectronic species
Same number of electrons: size increases as nuclear charge decreases. N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺. S²⁻ > Cl⁻ > K⁺ > Ca²⁺. Also Au⁺ > Hg²⁺ > Tl³⁺ > Pb⁴⁺.

Irregularities in the d-block

ElementScTiVCrMnFeCoNiCuZn
Radius (pm)144132122117117117116115117125
  • Size falls at first, stays almost constant from Cr to Cu (added d electrons screen the 4s electron from the nucleus), then rises slightly at Zn.
  • Group 3 shows the normal increase down the group. From group 4 to 12 the size rises from the 1st to the 2nd member but hardly changes from the 2nd to the 3rd (Zr ≈ Hf, Nb ≈ Ta, Pd ≈ Pt).
  • Lanthanide contraction: across the 14 lanthanides (Ce to Lu) the poorly shielding 4f electrons make the size shrink steadily. It cancels the normal increase down a group, so 5d elements are about as small as 4d elements.
Memory hooks
  • Cations shrink, anions swell. Think of losing weight versus gaining weight.
  • Same electrons, more protons means smaller. Z up, size down in any isoelectronic series.
  • Sizes: van der Waals > metallic > covalent. Remember "weak forces, large radius": non-bonded atoms sit farther apart than bonded ones.
  • Lanthanide contraction in one line: 4f electrons shield badly, so the 5d elements come out the same size as the 4d ones.
Traps and exceptions
  • Mg²⁺ is smaller than O²⁻ even though both have 10 electrons: Mg²⁺ has 12 protons, O²⁻ only 8.
  • A cation can be much smaller than its atom because a whole shell is lost, not just one electron (Li 1.23 Å, Li⁺ 0.76 Å).
  • Be²⁺ < Mg²⁺ in the size ordering: Be²⁺ has one shell, Mg²⁺ two.
  • Do not compare ions by charge alone; first compare the number of shells, then Z/e.
  • The same atom can have different radii (covalent, metallic, van der Waals). Quote the type when comparing.
  • In a bond with large EN difference the bond is shorter than the sum of covalent radii.
  • Radii do not decrease smoothly in the d-block. Cr to Cu are nearly equal and Zn is larger than Cu.
  • Noble gas radii are van der Waals and look large next to the covalent radii of their neighbours (F 72 pm, Ne 160 pm).
Worked example: arrange Be²⁺, Cl⁻, S²⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Br⁻ by increasing size

Be²⁺ (1 shell) < Mg²⁺ < Na⁺ (both 2 shells, isoelectronic, Mg has more protons) < Cl⁻ < S²⁻ (3 shells, isoelectronic) < Br⁻ (4 shells). So Be²⁺ < Mg²⁺ < Na⁺ < Cl⁻ < S²⁻ < Br⁻.

Worked example: iodine radius from C–I (bond length 2.13 Å, r(C) = 0.77 Å)

C and I have about equal electronegativity (2.5), so d = r_C + r_I and r_I = 2.13 − 0.77 = 1.36 Å.

Worked example: % of Li's volume taken by its 2s electron (r = 1.23 Å, r(Li⁺) = 0.76 Å)

V(Li) ∝ 1.23³ = 1.861; V(Li⁺) ∝ 0.76³ = 0.439. The 2s share = (1.861 − 0.439)/1.861 = 76.4%.

6 · Ionisation Energy

The big idea. Ionisation energy (IE) is the energy needed to pull the most loosely held electron out of an isolated gaseous atom. A small, high-Zeff atom with a stable configuration holds on tightly and has a high IE. Each successive IE is larger than the one before, with a huge jump once the valence electrons are gone and you start digging into the core.

Definitions and formulas

First, second, third IE
M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻ (IE₁)
M⁺ → M²⁺ + e⁻ is IE₂ (= IE₁ of M⁺) · M → M²⁺ + 2e⁻ costs IE₁ + IE₂
Order of successive IEs
IE₁ < IE₂ < IE₃ …
fewer electrons, same nuclear charge
Units
1 eV = 96.5 kJ/mol
kJ/mol, kcal/mol or eV per atom
Order of penetration
s > p > d > f
for the same shell, if other factors are equal, IE follows this order

Factors

FactorEffect on IE
Atomic sizeLarger atom: lower IE
Nuclear chargeMore charge: higher IE
ShieldingMore inner electrons: lower IE

Electronic configurationHalf-filled and fully filled subshells are extra stable: higher than expected

Reading successive IEs

PatternMeaning
IE₂ ≫ IE₁1 valence electron: alkali metal (ns¹)

Very high IEs throughoutNon-metal or noble gas
Biggest jump after IEₙThe atom has n valence electrons; the next electron comes from a core shell
Memory hooks
  • Period 2 in order: Li < B < Be < C < O < N < F < Ne. (Both dips: Be → B and N → O.)
  • Period 3 in order: Na < Al < Mg < S < Si < P < Cl < Ar.
  • Big jump = shell change. Count how many IEs are small before the jump; that is the number of valence electrons.
  • Cs has the lowest IE (of stable elements) but Li is the best reducing agent in water, because Li⁺ has the highest hydration energy (it is the smallest).
Traps and exceptions
  • IE is a gas-phase property, in kJ/mol of atoms. Do not mix with the aqueous-phase reducing power.
  • Removing electrons from lower-n orbitals is harder, not easier (core electrons are held much more tightly).
  • IE₂ of M is the IE₁ of M⁺. The energy for M → M²⁺ is the sum IE₁ + IE₂.
  • K vs Ca: IE₁ Ca > IE₁ K, but IE₂ K ≫ IE₂ Ca. K⁺ already has a noble-gas core.
  • The expected trend is not smooth. Check for filled or half-filled subshells (Be, Mg, N, P) before predicting.
  • Be and Mg (ns²) have high IE₁ but still lose both electrons easily, with IE₁ and IE₂ close and a big jump at IE₃.
  • Fluorine has the highest IE in its period? No: Ne does. Among F, O, N the answer is F, but with Ne or Ar the noble gas wins.
Worked example: classify I (403, 2640), II (549, 1060), III (1142, 2080) kJ/mol

I: lowest IE₁ and a huge jump to IE₂ means an alkali metal. II: IE₁ and IE₂ close, IE₁ slightly higher than I (ns² stability) means an alkaline earth metal. III: highest values throughout means a non-metal.

Worked example: energy to convert 12 mg of Mg vapour to Mg²⁺ (IE₁ = 7.646 eV, IE₂ = 15.035 eV)

Per atom: 7.646 + 15.035 = 22.681 eV = 22.681 × 96.5 = 2188.7 kJ/mol. 12 mg = 0.5 × 10⁻³ mol of Mg, so energy = 0.5×10⁻³ × 2188.7 = 1.094 kJ.

7 · Electron Gain Enthalpy

The big idea. Electron gain enthalpy (ΔegH) is the enthalpy change when a gaseous atom takes up an electron: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g). If energy is released, ΔegH is negative and the atom is "electron-hungry". Electron affinity (Ea) is the same number with the opposite sign. Halogens are the most eager; noble gases and filled-subshell atoms are not.

Formulas and conventions

Relationship
Ea = − ΔegH
same magnitude, opposite sign
Signs
Ea positive = energy released = exothermic = ΔegH negative. Ea negative = energy absorbed = endothermic = ΔegH positive.
Second electron gain
O⁻ + e⁻ → O²⁻ : +744 kJ/mol
always endothermic (repulsion beats attraction). First: O + e⁻ → O⁻ : −141 kJ/mol
Proportionality
Ea ∝ Zeff / size
also falls with more screening and with filled or half-filled subshells

Memory hooks
  • Order for halogens: Cl > F > Br > I (magnitude of the negative value).
  • Group 16: S > Se > Te > O in magnitude (oxygen is the odd one out).
  • Sign trick: "Negative ΔH means energy given out." A bigger negative ΔegH means a greater affinity for electrons.
  • Two-line summary: small + high Zeff is good for gaining electrons, unless the atom is so small that electrons repel (F, O, N) or the target subshell is full or half-full (Be, Mg, N, noble gases).
Traps and exceptions
  • Fluorine's EA is less than chlorine's, although F is the most electronegative element. Small size means heavy electron repulsion.
  • Second ΔegH is always positive. The first can be negative or positive.
  • The neutral atom has the highest affinity: for O, O⁰ > O⁻ > O²⁻, because an anion repels an incoming electron.
  • Be and Ne have positive ΔegH against the general trend: Be (2s²) must place the electron in 2p; Ne has a full shell.
  • Li to Be: the electron goes into 2s for Li but into 2p for Be, where it is poorly held, so ΔegH jumps from negative to positive.
  • Do not confuse "EA is positive" with "ΔegH is positive." They have opposite signs.
  • ΔegH of a cation: K⁺ + e⁻ → K has ΔegH = −IE₁(K), because it is the reverse of ionisation.
  • Mind the sign when comparing "highest EA": pick the most negative ΔegH.
Worked example: order N, P, O, S by increasing negative electron gain enthalpy

N (≈ 0 or positive) < P (−72) < O (−141) < S (−200). So N < P < O < S.

Worked example: 10⁶ iodine atoms release 5.0×10⁻¹³ J in forming I⁻

Energy per atom = 5.0×10⁻¹⁹ J. Per mole = 5.0×10⁻¹⁹ × 6.023×10²³ = 3.01×10⁵ J = 301 kJ released, so ΔegH = −301 kJ/mol. In eV per atom: 301/96.5 = −3.12 eV.

8 · Electronegativity

The big idea. Electronegativity (EN or χ) measures how strongly an atom attracts the shared electrons in a covalent bond. It cannot be measured directly, so scales are used. It depends on both ionisation energy and electron affinity: an atom that holds its own electrons tightly and also wants more is highly electronegative. Fluorine is the most electronegative element (4.0 on the Pauling scale).

Scales and formulas

Pauling scale (kcal/mol)
χA − χB = 0.208 √[EA–B − √(EA–A·EB–B)]
E = bond energy; A is the more electronegative atom
Pauling scale (kJ/mol)
χA − χB = 0.1017 √[EA–B − √(EA–A·EB–B)]
same formula with the constant for kJ/mol
Mulliken scale
χM = (IE + EA)/2
both in eV. Mulliken values are about 2.8 times Pauling's: χP ≈ χM/2.8
Relation (for reference only)
χP = 1.35 √χM − 1.37
the PDF says you need not memorise this

Applications

UseExample
Naming binary compounds: the less electronegative element is named first; the more electronegative gets "-ide".ICl iodine chloride · ClF chlorine fluoride · IBr iodine bromide · OF₂ oxygen difluoride · Cl₂O dichlorine oxide
Bond length: a greater ΔEN means a shorter bond.ΔEN: HF > HCl > HBr > HI, so bond length: HF < HCl < HBr < HI
Fixing oxidation statesThe more electronegative atom takes the negative state
Memory hooks
  • EN is a tug-of-war rope: the stronger puller (higher IE and EA) wins the shared electrons.
  • F > O > N, Cl > C, Br > S, I, P, H is a handy scrambled ranking: F 4.0, O 3.5, N 3.0 = Cl 3.0, Br 2.8, then C = S = I = 2.5, then P = H = 2.1.
  • s-character: an sp carbon (50% s) pulls electrons harder than an sp³ carbon (25% s).
  • Mulliken to Pauling: divide by 2.8.
Traps and exceptions
  • EN ≠ EA. Cl has the higher electron affinity, but F is more electronegative.
  • Careful with the order of Cl and O: O (3.5) > Cl (3.0), so "F > Cl > O > S" is wrong; the correct order is F > O > Cl > S.
  • N is more electronegative than C (3.0 vs 2.5), so "Be < B < N < C" is wrong.
  • Down group 1 EN falls: Li > Na > K > Rb > Cs. Cs is the least electronegative stable alkali metal.
  • EN changes with the oxidation state and hybridisation of the same atom. The Pauling value is for a typical state.
  • Br is 2.8 and Cl is 3.0, yet I and C are both 2.5, which is why C–I is treated as nearly non-polar.
  • Pauling's ΔEN uses the square root of the energy excess. Do not forget the outer square root of EA–A·EB–B.
Worked example: EN of F on the Pauling scale from IE = 17.42 eV and EA = 3.45 eV

χM = (17.42 + 3.45)/2 = 10.435. χP = 10.435/2.8 = 3.73 (the accepted value is 4.0, so this conversion is approximate).

Worked example: arrange N, P, C, Si by decreasing EN

N 3.0 > C 2.5 > P 2.1 > Si 1.8, i.e. N > C > P > Si.
