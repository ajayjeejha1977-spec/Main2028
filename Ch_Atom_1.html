3 · Bohr Model

The big idea. Rutherford's electron should spiral into the nucleus, but atoms are stable. Bohr fixed this with three rules:
  1. The electron moves only in special stationary orbits and radiates nothing while in one.
  2. Allowed orbits have angular momentum mvr = nh/2π (n = 1, 2, 3…).
  3. It changes orbit only by absorbing or emitting one photon with energy equal to the gap: ΔE = hν = hc/λ.
It works only for one-electron species (H, He⁺, Li²⁺, Be³⁺ …).
Where do the formulas come from? (30-second derivation)

Balance the pull of the nucleus with the centripetal force: mv²/r = KZe²/r² … (1)
Bohr's rule: mvr = nh/2π … (2)
Solve (1) and (2) together: r = n²h²/(4π²mKZe²) ∝ n²/Z and v = 2πKZe²/(nh) ∝ Z/n.
Energy: K.E. = ½mv² = KZe²/2r, P.E. = −KZe²/r, so T.E. = −KZe²/2r. Putting in r gives E = −13.6 Z²/n² eV.

Memory hooks
  • Power chart. Powers of n for (r, v, E, T, f) are 2, −1, −2, 3, −3. Powers of Z are −1, 1, 2, −2, 2. Learn these ten numbers and every ratio question becomes arithmetic.
  • Big Z pulls the electron in: smaller orbit, faster electron, more tightly bound (energy more negative), shorter period.
  • Two species look alike when… equal r needs equal n²/Z; equal v and equal E need equal Z/n. Example: H (n=1), He⁺ (n=2), Li²⁺ (n=3) all have the same v and the same E, but different radii.
  • Energy ladder: H −13.6, He⁺ −54.4 (×4), Li²⁺ −122.4 (×9), Be³⁺ −217.6 (×16) eV in the ground state.
Traps and exceptions
  • One electron only. Be²⁺, Li⁺, He have two electrons, so Bohr's formulas do not apply. Be³⁺ does.
  • "nth excited state" means n + 1. 1st excited = n = 2, 2nd excited = n = 3.
  • Energy goes up with n (becomes less negative). So n ↑ : T.E. ↑, P.E. ↑, K.E. ↓, speed ↓.
  • K.E. is positive and equals |T.E.|; P.E. is twice the (negative) T.E.
  • I.E. in eV and I.P. in volt are the same number (13.6 eV ↔ 13.6 V). Excitation energy of n = 2 is 10.2 eV and of n = 3 is 12.09 eV.
  • Bohr's angular momentum ≠ orbital angular momentum. The 4th Bohr orbit has 4h/2π, but a 4s orbital has L = 0, and 3p has √2 h/2π.
  • Heavier particle in place of the electron (muon, 207 mₑ): r ∝ 1/m shrinks, energy ∝ m so I.E. increases.
  • Failures of Bohr's model: multi-electron spectra, doublets (fine structure), Zeeman and Stark splitting, Heisenberg uncertainty, de Broglie waves, chemical bonding.
Worked example: ratio of radii of He⁺ (n = 2) and Be³⁺ (n = 3)

r ∝ n²/Z. He⁺: 4/2 = 2. Be³⁺: 9/4. Ratio = 2 ÷ 9/4 = 8/9.

Worked example: ratio of time periods, He⁺ (n = 2) to Li²⁺ (n = 4)

T ∝ n³/Z². He⁺: 8/4 = 2. Li²⁺: 64/9. Ratio = 2 × 9/64 = 9/32.

4 · Hydrogen Spectrum

The big idea. A hydrogen atom can only emit light when its electron drops from a higher level n₂ to a lower level n₁. The photon's energy is the gap between the levels, so only certain wavelengths appear: a line spectrum. The lower level n₁ decides the series. The same wavelengths appear as dark lines in an absorption spectrum.

Memory hooks
  • Order of series: Lazy Brown Pandas Break Pots Happily gives Lyman, Balmer, Paschen, Brackett, Pfund, Humphrey for n₁ = 1 to 6.
  • Regions: Lyman = UV, Balmer = visible, all the rest = IR. Higher n₁ means lower energy and longer wavelength.
  • First line = longest λ (smallest gap). Last line (series limit) = shortest λ (biggest gap).
  • He⁺ copies H: He⁺ transition (2n₁ → 2n₂) has the same λ as H (n₁ → n₂), because Z² = 4 cancels. Likewise Li²⁺ (3n₁ → 3n₂).
  • Wave number of Lyman lines: 3R/4, 8R/9, 15R/16 … always R(1 − 1/n²).
Traps and exceptions
  • Only four Balmer lines are visible (3→2, 4→2, 5→2, 6→2, from about 6563 Å down to 4102 Å). The rest of the Balmer series is UV. The printed notes give the first Balmer line as 6536 Å; the correct value is 6563 Å.
  • Single atom vs sample. "A single isolated atom at n = 4" gives at most 3 lines. "A sample of atoms" gives 6.
  • "nth excited state" is n + 1. From the 5th excited state (n = 6) to the ground state: 15 lines.
  • The series is decided by the lower level (the level the electron lands on).
  • H-like ions: do not forget Z². For He⁺ the Lyman series limit is 1/(4R), four times shorter in wavelength than H.
  • Counting IR lines means counting all transitions that land on n ≥ 3 (Paschen, Brackett, Pfund …).
  • Bohr's formulas fail for atoms with more than one electron, so this spectrum formula is only for H-like species.
Worked example: photon from n = 5 to n = 2 in H

ΔE = 2.18×10⁻¹⁸ (1/4 − 1/25) = 4.58×10⁻¹⁹ J. Frequency = ΔE/h = 6.91×10¹⁴ Hz. λ = c/ν = 434 nm, so it is visible (Balmer series, violet-blue).

Worked example: lines from the 7th excited state to the 2nd state

7th excited state is n = 8. Δn = 8 − 2 = 6, so lines = 6 × 7 / 2 = 21.

6 · Quantum Numbers & Electronic Configuration

The big idea. Every electron in an atom has an "address" of four numbers. Three come from the Schrödinger equation and describe the orbital (size, shape, orientation). The fourth is the electron's spin. Three rules (Aufbau, Pauli, Hund) decide which addresses get filled.

Memory hooks
  • Orbital counts: s, p, d, f have 1, 3, 5, 7 orbitals (odd numbers), holding 2, 6, 10, 14 electrons.
  • Node counting: radial = n − ℓ − 1. So ns has n − 1 radial nodes, np has n − 2, nd has n − 3. Total nodes are always n − 1.
  • Half-filled and fully filled are happy: p³, p⁶, d⁵, d¹⁰, f⁷, f¹⁴ are extra stable (less shielding, more exchange energy, less repulsion).
  • Fill by energy, remove by distance: 4s fills before 3d, but when an atom becomes an ion, the 4s electrons leave first.
  • Spin counting: every unpaired electron is worth ½. Five unpaired electrons give total spin ±5/2 and μ = 5.92 BM.
Traps and exceptions
  • Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, not 3d⁴ 4s² and 3d⁹ 4s². Within Z ≤ 36 these are the only two exceptions.
  • Ions of d-block elements lose 4s first. Fe³⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁵ (not 4s² 3d³). Cu⁺ = 3d¹⁰ (diamagnetic). Cr³⁺ = 3d³.
  • Allowed values: ℓ stops at n − 1 (no 2d, 1p, 3f). m stops at ±ℓ. The set (n=4, ℓ=3, m=4) is impossible; so is (n=1, ℓ=0, m=1).
  • Tie-break in (n + ℓ): 3d (5) comes before 4p (5) because its n is lower.
  • μ uses unpaired electrons, not the total electron count. A completely filled configuration has μ = 0.
  • The five d-orbitals are not alike: dz² has two conical nodes (not nodal planes) and a donut, while the other four have two nodal planes each.
  • Shell capacity: n = 2 holds 8, not 2² = 4. Remember 2n².
  • Valence electron of Rb (Z = 37) is 5s¹ (n=5, ℓ=0, m=0, s=+½), not 6s.
Worked example: quantum numbers of the 5th and 6th electrons of carbon

C = 1s² 2s² 2p². Both are in 2p, so n = 2, ℓ = 1. By Hund's rule they go in different p orbitals with parallel spins: 5th electron m = −1 (or +1), 6th electron m = 0 (spins both +½ or both −½).

Worked example: spin magnetic moment of Cr (Z = 24)

Cr = 3d⁵ 4s¹, so 6 unpaired electrons. μ = √(6 × 8) = √48 ≈ 6.93 BM. Total spin = ±3.

5 · de Broglie Waves & Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

The big idea. Light behaves as a wave and as a particle. de Broglie asked: if so, why can't matter, such as an electron, also behave like a wave? Every moving particle has a wavelength λ = h/mv. Davisson and Germer proved it by seeing electrons diffract. Heisenberg then showed a limit: you can never know both the exact position and the exact momentum of a tiny particle at the same time. That is why Bohr's neat circular orbits had to be replaced by probability regions (orbitals).

Memory hooks
  • λ = h/p. Every comparison question is about momentum. Write p first, then compare.
  • Light particles are the wavy ones. Ball: λ ≈ 10⁻³⁴ m (undetectable). Electron: about 1 Å, similar to atom size, so electron diffraction works.
  • Uncertainty is a trade-off. Squeeze one (Δx small) and the other (Δp) blows up. Think of an electron located by a very short-wavelength photon: that photon hits it hard and kicks it.
  • Three "h/4π"s: Δx·Δp, Δx·mΔv and ΔE·Δt all share the same right-hand side.
Traps and exceptions
  • 12.3/√V Å is for electrons only. For a proton or α, go back to λ = h/√(2mqV).
  • Heavy objects have no practical uncertainty. A 40 g golf ball with Δv = 0.9 m/s has Δx ≈ 1.5×10⁻³³ m: no meaningful limit. The principle matters only for electron-sized particles.
  • It is "≥" (minimum). The calculated Δx or Δv is the smallest possible uncertainty.
  • Same K.E. is not same speed. An electron and a proton with equal K.E. have λ ratio √(mₚ/mₑ) ≈ 43, not 1836.
  • Velocity-dependent mass. At speeds near c, use m = m₀/√(1 − v²/c²). A photon is never at rest and has m₀ = 0.
  • Neutrons carry no charge and cannot be accelerated by a voltage, so λ = h/√(2qmV) does not apply to them.
  • Uncertainty principle vs Bohr's orbits: a definite orbit means exact position and exact momentum together, which is forbidden. So the idea of orbits fails.
Worked example: λ of a 0.1 kg ball moving at 10 m/s

λ = h/mv = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ / (0.1 × 10) = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ m. This is far too small to detect, so we never see ball waves.

Worked example: golf ball (40 g, 45 m/s, measured to 2%)

Δv = 2% of 45 = 0.9 m/s. Δx = h/(4π m Δv) = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ / (4π × 0.04 × 0.9) = 1.46×10⁻³³ m, about 10¹⁸ times smaller than a nucleus.

7 · Quantum Mechanical Model: Orbitals & Nodes

The big idea. Schrödinger treated the electron as a wave and wrote an equation whose solutions are wave functions ψ. ψ itself means nothing physical, but ψ² is the probability density of finding the electron at a point. An orbital is the region of space where that probability is highest (about 90–95%). The solution automatically produces the quantum numbers n, ℓ and m.

Memory hooks
  • Nodes recipe: total = n − 1; angular = ℓ; radial is what is left. For s orbitals all nodes are radial, so ns has n − 1 of them and np has n − 2.
  • More nodes = more energy within a shell (that is why s < p < d < f in a many-electron atom).
  • Shape ladder: s sphere, p dumb-bell (2 lobes), d double dumb-bell (4 lobes), f leaf-like (8 lobes).
  • ψ² can't be negative. That is why the square is used as the probability, while ψ can be + or −.
Traps and exceptions
  • Radial nodes and radial peaks are not the same number. 2s has 1 radial node but 2 peaks in 4πr²R².
  • Density at the nucleus vs radial probability. The ψ² (density) of 1s is maximum at the nucleus, but 4πr²R² (probability in a shell) is zero there because the shell volume is zero.
  • In hydrogen, energy depends on n only (2s and 2p have the same energy). In many-electron atoms it depends on (n + ℓ), because s electrons penetrate closer to the nucleus.
  • dz² is the odd one out among the d orbitals: its nodes are conical surfaces, not planes, and it has a donut ring in the xy-plane.
  • Orbital ≠ orbit. An orbital holds at most 2 electrons, an orbit up to 2n².
  • ψ has no physical meaning, only ψ² does. A positive or negative sign on a lobe is not a charge.
  • ℓ must be less than n. 1p, 2d and 3f do not exist.
Worked example: nodes in a 3p orbital

Radial = n − ℓ − 1 = 3 − 1 − 1 = 1. Angular = ℓ = 1. Total = n − 1 = 2 (one radial, one angular).

Worked example: which orbital has 2 radial nodes and 1 angular node?

ℓ = 1 (p). Radial = n − 2 = 2, so n = 4. It is a 4p orbital.

8 · Nuclear Chemistry

The big idea. Some nuclei are unstable and fall apart on their own, sending out α, β or γ rays. This is radioactivity, a property of the nucleus only. Heat, pressure, physical state or chemical combination make no difference. Which way a nucleus decays depends on its neutron-to-proton (n/p) ratio and its size.

Memory hooks
  • Count α first, then fix with β. α count = (A − A′)/4. Then β⁻ count = 2 × (α count) − (Z − Z′).
  • High n/p → β⁻ (neutron turns into proton). Low n/p → β⁺ or EC (proton turns into neutron). Heavy → α.
  • Compare with the stable isotope. If your isotope is heavier than the stable one of the same element, it has too many neutrons, so β⁻. If lighter, β⁺ or EC. (Na-24 is heavy, Na-22 light, Na-23 stable.)
  • Both β⁺ and EC give the same product, with Z lowered by 1 and A unchanged.
  • Arrow directions: α goes left 2, β⁻ goes right 1. One α plus one β⁻ gives net one place left.
Traps and exceptions
  • β⁻ is not an orbital electron. It is created inside the nucleus when n → p + e⁻.
  • γ emission changes neither Z nor A. It only releases extra nuclear energy (e.g. ₄₃Tc⁹⁹ metastable → ₄₃Tc⁹⁹).
  • Radioactivity is independent of chemical state. ¹⁴C is radioactive in C, CO₂ or any compound.
  • Fission splits a heavy nucleus; fusion joins light ones. Both release energy because some mass turns into energy.
  • Odd–odd nuclei are rare (only 5 stable ones, such as ₁H², ₃Li⁶, ₅B¹⁰, ₇N¹⁴), not the common type.
  • Be careful with β⁺ questions. When a question says "a charged particle is emitted" in a textbook problem, check whether positrons are allowed; the PDF's problem assumes only α and β⁻.
  • Two typos in the PDF. It gives n − p = 10 for ₉F¹⁹ and ₁₉K³⁹, but it is 1. It also prints "₁₁N²³" where it means ₁₁Na²³.
  • Fission is defined in the PDF as splitting into "large nuclei"; it means medium-sized, smaller ones.
Worked example: how does ₁₁Na²⁴ decay? (Na-23 is the stable isotope)

n/p = 13/11, which is above 1 and above Na-23's 12/11. Too many neutrons, so β⁻ emission: ₁₁Na²⁴ → ₁₂Mg²⁴ + ₋₁e⁰.

Worked example: α and β particles in ₉₂U²³⁸ → ₈₂Pb²⁰⁶

α count = (238 − 206)/4 = 8. β⁻ count = 2 × 8 − (92 − 82) = 16 − 10 = 6.

Worked example: decay of ₇N¹³

Stable nitrogen is N-14, so N-13 has fewer neutrons. n/p = 6/7, which is low. It is a positron emitter: ₇N¹³ → ₆C¹³ + ₊₁e⁰.

1 · Sub-atomic Particles & Early Atomic Models

The big idea. Dalton said the atom is indivisible. Discharge-tube experiments disproved that: atoms are built from electrons, protons and neutrons. Thomson pictured a "plum pudding". Rutherford's gold-foil experiment replaced it with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space. Rutherford's model then failed (the electron should spiral into the nucleus), which led to Bohr.

Memory hooks
  • Who found what: electron → Thomson (cathode), proton → Goldstein (anode), neutron → Chadwick (the Charge-less one).
  • Cathode vs anode e/m: cathode rays are always electrons, so e/m never changes. Anode rays are whatever positive ion the gas gives, so e/m changes with the gas.
  • isoTOPE, isoBAR, isoTONE: topes share the top number Z (protons), bars share the bulk (mass A), tones share the neutrons (the "n" in tone).
  • Nuclear density is the same for all nuclei because volume ∝ R³ ∝ A, and mass ∝ A.
Traps and exceptions
  • Hydrogen (protium) has no neutron. Every other atom does.
  • Ions change only the electrons. The nucleus is untouched: Ca²⁺ has 20 protons and 18 electrons; Br⁻ has 35 protons and 36 electrons.
  • Mass number is always a whole number; atomic mass usually is not.
  • Proton and electron charges are equal and opposite. The proton is not "more charged".
  • Closest approach depends on the α energy, not just speed: R ∝ 1/K.E. Double the energy and R halves; a higher-Z nucleus gives a larger R.
  • Isotopes share chemical properties but differ in mass-dependent physical properties.
  • Isobars are different elements (different Z), so they have different chemical properties.
  • Anode rays do not come out of the anode metal. They form by ionising gas and stream through the cathode's holes.
Worked example: protons, neutrons and electrons in the bromide ion (36 electrons, 45 neutrons)

Br⁻ has gained 1 electron, so the Br atom has 36 − 1 = 35 electrons = 35 protons. Z = 35, A = 35 + 45 = 80.

Worked example: nuclear radius ratio for A = 64 and A = 8

R ∝ A^(1/3). R₆₄ : R₈ = 4 : 2 = 2 : 1.

2 · Light & the Photoelectric Effect

The big idea. Interference and diffraction show light is a wave. But black-body radiation, the photoelectric effect and the hydrogen line spectrum could not be explained by waves. Planck and Einstein fixed this: light comes in packets of energy called photons, each with E = hν. In the photoelectric effect, one photon knocks out one electron. Light itself behaves as both wave and particle.

Wave basics

Speed relation
c = ν λ
c = 3×10⁸ m/s in vacuum for all EM waves, any λ

EM wave
oscillating E and B fields, perpendicular to each other; needs no medium

Memory hooks
  • Frequency decides energy; intensity decides number. This one line answers most photoelectric questions.
  • Ticket analogy. W₀ is the ticket price. A photon pays once and any change is the electron's K.E. Many cheap photons cannot add up to a ticket.
  • K.E. vs ν graph: a straight line with slope h and x-intercept ν₀. All metals give parallel lines; only the intercept changes.
  • Spectrum order (increasing energy): Rabbits Mate In Very Unusual eXpensive Gardens = Radio, Microwave, Infrared, Visible, Ultraviolet, X-ray, Gamma.
  • Quick conversions: λ in nm and E in eV: E = 1240/λ. Green light (500 nm) is about 2.5 eV.
Traps and exceptions
  • Below threshold frequency there is no emission, however intense the light. Even a billion photons of too-low energy do nothing.
  • Doubling the intensity doubles the electron count but leaves K.E. unchanged.
  • Doubling the frequency does not double the K.E. K.E. = hν − W₀, so it more than doubles. The photon energy is what doubles.
  • Threshold wavelength λ₀ is the longest wavelength that works. Emission needs λ ≤ λ₀.
  • The K.E. in the equation is the maximum. Electrons that lose energy inside the metal come out slower.
  • Units. With h in J·s, use λ in metres and get joules. For eV answers, use 12400 eV·Å (λ in Å).
  • Visible light spans only 400–750 nm (about 1.65–3.1 eV). UV photons are more energetic than visible ones.
  • All EM waves travel at c in vacuum, whatever their wavelength.
  • Photons have zero rest mass but carry momentum p = h/λ.
  • Planck's theory also explains other failures of classical physics: black-body radiation (Rayleigh–Jeans predicted infinite intensity), heat capacity of solids at low temperature, and the line spectrum of hydrogen.
Worked example: K.E. when ν₀ = 6×10¹⁴ s⁻¹ and ν = 1.1×10¹⁵ s⁻¹

K.E. = h(ν − ν₀) = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 5×10¹⁴ = 3.313×10⁻¹⁹ J (about 2.07 eV).

Worked example: longest wavelength to decompose AgCl (248 kJ/mol)

One photon breaks one AgCl unit: N_A·hc/λ = 248×10³ J. λ = (6.626×10⁻³⁴ × 3×10⁸ × 6.023×10²³)/(248×10³) = 4.83×10⁻⁷ m = 483 nm, which is visible light.

Worked example: 620 nm light on a metal with W₀ = 1.5 eV

E = 1240/620 = 2.0 eV. K.E.max = 2.0 − 1.5 = 0.5 eV. Stopping potential = 0.5 V.
