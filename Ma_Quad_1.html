Quadratic Equations – Remember Sheet

Formulas, conditions and tricks from the module, grouped to match the CBT topics. Notation: \(D=b^2-4ac\), \(S=\alpha+\beta\), \(P=\alpha\beta\).

1 · Roots: Sum & Product

  1. \(\alpha+\beta=-\dfrac ba,\ \ \alpha\beta=\dfrac ca\); roots \(\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt D}{2a}\); \(|\alpha-\beta|=\dfrac{\sqrt D}{|a|}\).
  2. Equation from roots: \(x^2-Sx+P=0\).
  3. \(a+b+c=0\Rightarrow\) root \(1\) (other \(c/a\)). \(a-b+c=0\Rightarrow\) root \(-1\). \(a=c\Rightarrow\) roots are reciprocals. \(b=0\Rightarrow\) roots are equal and opposite.
  4. Example: \((b-c)x^2+(c-a)x+(a-b)=0\) has roots \(1,\ \frac{a-b}{b-c}\).
  5. Mis-copied coefficient: the correct coefficient gives the true sum (or product); the other one is taken from the faulty roots (PT1 Q4: roots \(8,3\)).

2 · Symmetric Functions & Recurrence

  1. \(\alpha^2+\beta^2=\dfrac{b^2-2ac}{a^2}\); \(\alpha^3+\beta^3=\dfrac{3abc-b^3}{a^3}\); \(\alpha^4+\beta^4=\dfrac{(b^2-2ac)^2-2a^2c^2}{a^4}\); \((\alpha-\beta)^2=\dfrac{D}{a^2}\).
  2. Each root satisfies the equation, so \(\alpha^{n+2}=p\alpha^{n+1}-q\alpha^n\) for \(x^2-px+q=0\). Hence \(S_n=pS_{n-1}-qS_{n-2}\), and the same for \(a_n=\alpha^n-\beta^n\) (use this to simplify ratios like \(\frac{a_{10}-2a_8}{2a_9}\)).
  3. A function is symmetric if it is unchanged when \(\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta\): \(\alpha^2\beta+\alpha\beta^2\) and \(\cos(\alpha-\beta)\) are; \(\alpha^2-\beta\) and \(\ln\frac\alpha\beta\) are not.
  4. Cubic: \(\sum\alpha=-\frac ba,\ \sum\alpha\beta=\frac ca,\ \alpha\beta\gamma=-\frac da\). Newton sums with \(e_1,e_2,e_3\): \(p_2=e_1p_1-2e_2\), \(p_3=e_1p_2-e_2p_1+3e_3\), \(p_4=e_1p_3-e_2p_2+e_3p_1\).
  5. Roots transformed (e.g. squares): substitute \(y=x^2\) and eliminate \(x\). For \(x^3+8=0\) the squares satisfy \(y^3-64=0\).
  6. Large powers of complex roots: use polar form (\(1\pm i=\sqrt2e^{\pm i\pi/4}\)) or \(\omega^3=1,\ 1+\omega+\omega^2=0\).

3 · Nature of Roots

  1. \(D>0\): real, distinct. \(D=0\): equal (\(-\frac b{2a}\)). \(D<0\): non-real conjugates (real coefficients).
  2. Rational coefficients: \(D\) a perfect square \(\Rightarrow\) rational roots; \(D>0\) non-square \(\Rightarrow\) surd pair \(p\pm\sqrt q\). Real coefficients and root \(2+i\sqrt3\Rightarrow\) \(x^2-4x+7=0\).
  3. \(ax^2+bx+c>0\ \forall x\iff a>0,\ D<0\). \(\ge0\ \forall x\iff a>0,\ D\le0\). \(<0\ \forall x\iff a<0,\ D<0\). If \(a\) holds a parameter, test \(a=0\) separately.
  4. Both roots positive: \(D\ge0,S>0,P>0\). Both negative: \(D\ge0,S<0,P>0\). Opposite signs: \(P<0\).
  5. Roots in ratio \(m:n\): \(mn\,b^2=(m+n)^2ac\). One root double the other: \(2b^2=9ac\). One root the square of the other: \(b^3+a^2c+ac^2=3abc\). One root the \(n\)th power of the other: \((ac^n)^{\frac1{n+1}}+(a^nc)^{\frac1{n+1}}+b=0\).

4 · Common Roots

  1. One common root: \((c_1a_2-c_2a_1)^2=(a_1b_2-a_2b_1)(b_1c_2-b_2c_1)\). Both common: \(\frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}=\frac{c_1}{c_2}\).
  2. In practice: subtract the equations to get the common root, then substitute (\(x^2-11x+a,\ x^2-14x+2a\Rightarrow x=\frac a3\Rightarrow a=0,24\)).
  3. Real-coefficient quadratics sharing one non-real root share both (so coefficients are proportional).
  4. If the other roots are constrained, write them via sum or product of each equation (common root \(r\) in \(x^2-6x+a\) gives other root \(6-r\); in \(x^2-cx+6\) gives \(\frac6r\)).

5 · Location of Roots

For \(f(x)=ax^2+bx+c\) with \(a>0\); vertex \(h=-\frac b{2a}\).

  1. Both roots \(>k\): \(D\ge0,\ h>k,\ f(k)>0\). Both \(0\).
  2. \(k\) lies between the roots: \(af(k)<0\) (no \(D\) condition needed).
  3. Both roots in \((k_1,k_2)\): \(D\ge0,\ k_10,\ af(k_2)>0\).
  4. Exactly one root in \((k_1,k_2)\): \(f(k_1)f(k_2)<0\) (check end-point cases separately). One root in each of two intervals: apply sign of \(f\) at the three points (e.g. \(f(1)>0,f(2)<0,f(3)>0\)).
  5. At least one positive root: \(P<0\), or \(P\ge0,\ S>0,\ D\ge0\). Roots on either side of the origin: \(ac<0\).

6 · Quadratic & Rational Inequalities

  1. \((x-\alpha)(x-\beta)<0\) (\(\alpha<\beta\)): between the roots; \(>0\): outside.
  2. Wavy curve: factorise, mark zeros, start with \(+\) on the right for a positive leading coefficient. Even-power factors do not change sign. Denominator zeros are always excluded; numerator zeros are included for \(\ge\). Example: \(\frac{(x-1)^4(x-3)^3}{(x-5)^2}\ge0\Rightarrow\{1\}\cup[3,5)\cup(5,\infty)\).
  3. When cancelling a factor, keep its zero excluded. A factor that is always positive (\(D<0\), \(a>0\)) can be dropped.

7 · Range & Extrema

  1. Minimum (a>0) \(=-\frac D{4a}\) at \(x=-\frac b{2a}\). On \([m,n]\): compare the vertex (if inside) with both end-points.
  2. Range of \(y=\frac{a_1x^2+b_1x+c_1}{a_2x^2+b_2x+c_2}\): cross-multiply to a quadratic in \(x\) and impose \(D_x\ge0\). Treat a vanishing \(x^2\) coefficient separately, and drop values lost to a cancelled common factor.
  3. Real \(x,y\) with a relation: view it as a quadratic in one variable, \(D\ge0\) gives the range of the other (e.g. \(x^2+y^2=6x-8y\Rightarrow x\in[-2,8],\ y\in[-9,1]\)).
  4. \(x+y+z=s,\ x^2+y^2+z^2=q\): use \((s-x)^2\le2(q-x^2)\). For \(s=4,q=6\): \(x\in[\frac23,2]\).
  5. Sum of squares of roots \(=S^2-2P\) as a function of the parameter, then minimise. Writing \(\frac{N}{D}=1+\frac{\cdot}{D}\) reduces ratios to the extreme of the denominator.
  6. \(x+\frac1x\ge2\) for \(x>0\), \(\le-2\) for \(x<0\); so \(|x+\frac1x|\ge2\).

8 · Modulus

  1. \(|x|a\iff x<-a\) or \(x>a\). \(|f|<|g|\iff(f-g)(f+g)<0\).
  2. \(|u+v|=|u|+|v|\iff uv\ge0\); \(|u-v|=|u|+|v|\iff uv\le0\). Use this when two moduli add to the sum of their insides (\(|x^2-1|+|2-x^2|=1\Rightarrow1\le x^2\le2\)).
  3. \(|f|=f\iff f\ge0\); \(|f|=-f\iff f\le0\).
  4. Otherwise break at the critical points, solve each interval, and keep only answers inside that interval.
  5. With \(x^2=|x|^2\): put \(t=|x|\ (t\ge0)\). Example \(x^2-|x|-12=0\Rightarrow t=4\Rightarrow x=\pm4\).
  6. Sum of non-negative terms \(=0\Rightarrow\) each is \(0\) (\(|x^2-1|+\sqrt{x^2-3x+2}=0\Rightarrow x=1\)).

9 · Irrational, Exponential & Log

  1. \(\sqrt f0,\ fg\iff(g<0,f\ge0)\) or \((g\ge0,f>g^2)\). \(\sqrt f=g\iff g\ge0,\ f=g^2\).
  2. Squaring can add roots: always verify in the original equation.
  3. When the same expression sits inside and outside a root, set \(y=\sqrt{\ \cdot\ }\) (\(x^2+18x+30=2\sqrt{x^2+18x+45}\Rightarrow y=5\)).
  4. Exponentials: put \(t=a^x\) with \(t>0\) and reject non-positive \(t\). Symmetric \(e^{4x}\ldots e^{x}\) equations: divide by \(e^{2x}\) and put \(t=e^x+e^{-x}\ge2\).
  5. \(x^2+\frac1{x^2}=t^2-2\) with \(t=x+\frac1x\), and \(|t|\ge2\) is needed for real \(x\).

10 · JEE Hard Habits

  1. Repeated root \(\alpha\) of \(ax^2+bx+c\): \(D=0\) and \(\alpha=-\frac b{2a}\). Use that \(\alpha\) also satisfies the second equation.
  2. Polynomial relations: compare degrees and coefficients; an integer-coefficient polynomial has \((m-n)\mid p(m)-p(n)\).
  3. "Sum of integers k for which there are no real roots": find \(D<0\) as an interval, list the integers, then check no extraneous root (e.g. \(x=1,2\) in a rational equation).
  4. Parameters in rational equations: clear denominators first, impose \(D\), then verify the excluded points.
  5. Two-condition problems (e.g. \(p+q=3,\ p^4+q^4=369\)): reduce to one unknown (\(pq\)), then use reality (\(D\ge0\)) to pick the right value.
  6. Before finishing: re-check the domain, the end-points (open or closed) and the edge case \(a=0\).
