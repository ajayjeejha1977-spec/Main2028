Module 1

What is chemical equilibrium?

Reversible reactions, dynamic equilibrium, and the types of equilibria.

After this module you can:
  • tell reversible from irreversible reactions, and explain why a closed vessel matters
  • explain what "dynamic" equilibrium means, and why rates (not concentrations) are equal
  • list the standard features of an equilibrium state
  • classify an equilibrium as physical or chemical, homogeneous or heterogeneous

1.1 The big idea: a two-way escalator

Imagine two floors of a mall joined by one up-escalator and one down-escalator. In the morning everyone is on the ground floor, so only the up-escalator is busy. As the first floor fills, people start coming down too. After a while the number going up each minute equals the number coming down each minute. The head-count on each floor stops changing, but people never stop moving.

A reversible reaction behaves the same way. Reactants turn into products (forward reaction). Products turn back into reactants (backward reaction). Eventually the two speeds become equal.

Equilibrium in one line At equilibrium: rate of forward reaction = rate of backward reaction. Because of this, the amounts of every substance stay constant, even though both reactions are still going on.

1.4 Features of the equilibrium state

  1. Dynamic. Both reactions keep going at equal speed. Nothing visible changes, but nothing has stopped.
  2. Constant macroscopic properties. Concentration, pressure, colour, density and similar properties stay fixed with time.
  3. Reachable from both sides. Starting from pure reactants or from the equivalent amount of pure products gives the same equilibrium mixture (same temperature and volume).
  4. Needs a closed system. Nothing can enter or leave.
  5. A catalyst does not change the equilibrium. It speeds up both directions equally, so the same state is reached sooner. It does not change composition or the equilibrium constant.
  6. \(\Delta G = 0\) at equilibrium. (\(\Delta G^\circ\) is usually not zero. More in Module 8.)
  7. It can be disturbed. Change concentration, pressure, volume or temperature and the system shifts to a new equilibrium (Le Chatelier, Module 9).
ADVANCED EXTRA: proving it is dynamic with isotopes

Take an equilibrium mixture of \(\mathrm{H_2}\), \(\mathrm{I_2}\) and \(\mathrm{HI}\). Add some \(\mathrm{D_2}\) (heavy hydrogen). Soon the mixture contains \(\mathrm{HD}\) and \(\mathrm{DI}\). The only way this can happen is if \(\mathrm{HI}\) keeps breaking apart and re-forming all the time. Check question 5 below uses the same idea.

1.5 Equilibrium is not the same as steady state

Two situations can look "constant" but are not true equilibrium:

  • A metal rod with one end in a furnace and the other end in ice. After a while each point on the rod has a fixed temperature. Heat is flowing in one direction only, constantly supplied and removed.
  • A tank with a tap filling it and a drain emptying it at the same rate. The level is fixed, but water goes in one direction only.

These are steady states, which need a continuous outside supply to be maintained. In a true dynamic equilibrium, flow takes place in both directions equally inside a closed system.

1.7 Common traps

  • "Equilibrium means equal amounts." No. It means equal rates. The amounts depend on the reaction.
  • "The reaction has stopped." No. Both reactions continue. Only the net change is zero.
  • "A catalyst gives more product." No. It only shortens the time to reach equilibrium.
  • "Any constant reading means equilibrium." Not so. Check whether the system is closed and whether flow is in both directions (steady state vs equilibrium).

1.8 Check yourself

Module 1 recap
  • Reversible reaction: both directions happen; closed vessel needed.
  • Equilibrium: \(r_f=r_b\), so concentrations are constant. It is dynamic, not stopped.
  • Same equilibrium from either side. A catalyst only saves time.
  • Physical vs chemical; homogeneous vs heterogeneous.
  • Steady state (one-way flow) is not equilibrium.
Module 2

Law of mass action, Kc and Kp

How to write the equilibrium constant, and how to convert between Kc and Kp.

After this module you can:
  • state the law of mass action and the meaning of "active mass"
  • write Kc and Kp for any reaction, including ones with solids, liquids and ions
  • find \(\Delta n_g\) correctly and convert between Kp and Kc
  • state the units of Kc and Kp

2.1 Law of mass action

Two molecules can only react if they meet. The more crowded the container, the more often they meet. Guldberg and Waage turned this into a law:

Law of mass action At a fixed temperature, the rate of a reaction is proportional to the product of the active masses of the reactants, each raised to the power of its coefficient in the balanced equation.

Active mass is a measure of "how crowded" a substance is:

  • for a dissolved substance or a gas (in Kc): its molar concentration, \(\dfrac{\text{moles}}{\text{volume in litres}}\)
  • for a gas (in Kp): its partial pressure
  • for a pure solid or pure liquid: taken as 1. Its concentration is fixed by its density, so adding more of it does not make the reaction "more crowded".

2.2 From rates to the equilibrium constant

Take \(\mathrm{aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD}\). If we treat it as a single-step reaction:

\[ r_f=k_f[A]^a[B]^b,\qquad r_b=k_b[C]^c[D]^d \]

At equilibrium \(r_f=r_b\), so

\[ \frac{k_f}{k_b}=\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}=K_c \]

At a fixed temperature \(k_f\) and \(k_b\) are constants, so their ratio is a constant. That constant is the equilibrium constant, and the concentrations used are the equilibrium ones.

ADVANCED EXTRA: what if the reaction is not a single step?

For a multi-step reaction the rate laws do not follow the coefficients, so the \(k_f/k_b\) argument above is only a model. Even then the expression for Kc keeps exactly this form. This is proved from thermodynamics (Module 8), and for a multi-step mechanism K equals the product of the \(k_f/k_b\) ratios of the individual steps.

The rule for writing K \[ K_c=\frac{[\text{products}]^{\text{coeff}}}{[\text{reactants}]^{\text{coeff}}}\qquad K_p=\frac{(p_{\text{products}})^{\text{coeff}}}{(p_{\text{reactants}})^{\text{coeff}}} \] Use equilibrium values only. Kp needs at least one gas.

2.3 Rules for writing the expression

  1. Products go on top, reactants at the bottom.
  2. Each term is raised to the power of its coefficient.
  3. Leave out pure solids and pure liquids (their value is 1).
  4. Include gases and dissolved species. In dilute aqueous solutions, water is the solvent and is left out.
  5. Kp uses partial pressures of gases only; if no gas is present, Kp is not defined.
  6. Change the way the equation is written and K changes (more in Module 4).

2.4 Relation between Kp and Kc

For an ideal gas, \(pV=nRT\), so \(p=\dfrac{n}{V}RT=CRT\), where \(C\) is the molar concentration. So each partial pressure is its concentration times \(RT\):

\[ K_p=\frac{(C_CRT)^c(C_DRT)^d}{(C_ART)^a(C_BRT)^b}=\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}\,(RT)^{(c+d)-(a+b)} \]
Key relation \[ K_p=K_c\,(RT)^{\Delta n_g},\qquad \Delta n_g=\text{(moles of gaseous products)}-\text{(moles of gaseous reactants)} \] Count only gases. \(\Delta n_g\) can be positive, negative, zero or a fraction. If \(\Delta n_g=0\) then \(K_p=K_c\). Use \(R=0.0821\ \mathrm{L\,atm\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}\) when Kc is in mol/L and Kp in atm. T must be in kelvin.

Kp calculator

Units Kc has units \((\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}})^{\Delta n_g}\) and Kp has units \((\mathrm{atm})^{\Delta n_g}\). For \(\Delta n_g=0\) there are no units. Strictly, K is a pure number because it is built from activities, but exam questions often ask for units, so state them unless told otherwise.

2.5 Worked examples

Example 1. One mole of acetic acid and one mole of ethanol are mixed in a container. At equilibrium 2/3 mol of ester has formed. Find Kc for \(\mathrm{CH_3COOH(l)+C_2H_5OH(l)\rightleftharpoons CH_3COOC_2H_5(l)+H_2O(l)}\).

  1. Make an ICE table (moles). Initial: acid 1, alcohol 1, ester 0, water 0. Change: \(-x,-x,+x,+x\) with \(x=\tfrac23\).
  2. At equilibrium: acid \(=\tfrac13\), alcohol \(=\tfrac13\), ester \(=\tfrac23\), water \(=\tfrac23\) mol.
  3. \(\Delta n=0\) (same number of moles on both sides), so the volume \(V\) cancels. We can use moles directly.
  4. \(K_c=\dfrac{(2/3)(2/3)}{(1/3)(1/3)}=\mathbf{4}\) (no units).

Example 2. For \(\mathrm{PCl_5(g)\rightleftharpoons PCl_3(g)+Cl_2(g)}\), Kc = 0.04 mol/L at 500 K. Find Kp.

  1. \(\Delta n_g=(1+1)-1=+1\).
  2. \(RT=0.0821\times500=41.05\).
  3. \(K_p=K_c(RT)^{1}=0.04\times41.05=\mathbf{1.64\ atm}\).
  4. Sense check: \(\Delta n_g>0\) and \(RT>1\), so Kp must be larger than Kc. It is.

Example 3. A closed vessel at total pressure 2 atm holds 2 mol \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\), 1 mol \(\mathrm{PCl_3}\) and 1 mol \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\) at equilibrium. Find Kp, and then Kc at 400 K.

  1. Total moles \(=4\). Mole fractions: \(\mathrm{PCl_5}=\tfrac24=0.5\), \(\mathrm{PCl_3}=\mathrm{Cl_2}=\tfrac14=0.25\).
  2. Partial pressure \(=\) mole fraction \(\times P_{total}\): \(\mathrm{PCl_5}=1\) atm, \(\mathrm{PCl_3}=0.5\) atm, \(\mathrm{Cl_2}=0.5\) atm.
  3. \(K_p=\dfrac{0.5\times0.5}{1}=\mathbf{0.25\ atm}\).
  4. \(\Delta n_g=+1\), \(RT=0.0821\times400=32.84\).
  5. \(K_c=\dfrac{K_p}{RT}=\dfrac{0.25}{32.84}\approx\mathbf{7.6\times10^{-3}\ mol/L}\).

2.6 Common traps

  • Using initial values in K. Only equilibrium values belong there.
  • Writing solids or pure liquids in the expression. Leave them out.
  • Counting solids/liquids in \(\Delta n_g\). Only gases count.
  • Using \(R=8.314\) with Kp in atm. Use 0.0821, unless the question works in other units.
  • Forgetting that coefficients become powers, e.g. \([\mathrm{NH_3}]^2\) for \(2\mathrm{NH_3}\).
  • Using Celsius in \(RT\). Always convert to kelvin.

2.7 Check yourself

Module 2 recap
  • Law of mass action: rate \(\propto\) product of active masses to the power of coefficients.
  • \(K=\dfrac{[\text{products}]^{\text{coeff}}}{[\text{reactants}]^{\text{coeff}}}\) at equilibrium; solids and pure liquids are left out.
  • \(K_p=K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}\); count gases only; T in kelvin.
  • \(\Delta n_g=0\Rightarrow K_p=K_c\). Units are (conc)Δn and (atm)Δn.
Module 3

Reaction quotient (Q): direction and extent

Which way will a mixture move, and how far does a reaction go?

After this module you can:
  • write Q and compare it with K to predict the direction of a reaction
  • judge the extent of a reaction from the size of K
  • explain why K tells you "how far" but not "how fast"

3.1 Q is a snapshot, K is the finishing line

Think of a GPS. K is the destination. Q is where you are right now. If you are short of the destination you keep driving forward; if you have overshot you must come back. A reaction mixture does the same, and it stops moving (on average) only when Q reaches K.

Reaction quotient For \(\mathrm{aA+bB\rightleftharpoons cC+dD}\): \[ Q_c=\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}\qquad Q_p=\frac{p_C^{\,c}\,p_D^{\,d}}{p_A^{\,a}\,p_B^{\,b}} \] Same form as K, but with the concentrations (or pressures) at any moment, not necessarily at equilibrium. Solids and pure liquids are left out, exactly as in K.
The decision rule
  • Q < K → too many reactants, too few products → the reaction goes forward (Q rises towards K).
  • Q > K → too many products → the reaction goes backward (Q falls towards K).
  • Q = K → the mixture is at equilibrium. No net change.

Memory trick: Q is on top of the comparison. If the numerator (products) is too small, Q is small, so we need to make more products.

Q changes continuously as the reaction runs, but K stays fixed (at a given temperature). Q travels towards K and then stops. In the Module 1 simulator, the "[B]/[A]" box is exactly Q, and "kf/kb" is K.

3.3 Extent of reaction: what the size of K tells you

Size of K (rough guide)What it means
\(K>10^{3}\)Products far outnumber reactants. The reaction is "product favoured" and almost complete. Reactant amounts can often be neglected.
\(10^{-3}<K<10^{3}\)Significant amounts of both reactants and products at equilibrium.
\(K<10^{-3}\)Reactants far outnumber products. The reaction is "reactant favoured" and hardly proceeds.
K tells you "how far", never "how fast" A huge K does not mean a quick reaction. Diamond turns into graphite with a favourable K at room temperature, yet you can wear a diamond for a lifetime. Speed depends on the activation energy (kinetics), not on K.
Quick rule for \(\mathrm{A\rightleftharpoons B}\) starting from pure A Fraction of A converted at equilibrium \(=\dfrac{K}{1+K}\). For \(K=4\) that is 80%; for \(K=0.01\) it is only about 1%.
ADVANCED EXTRA: one rule for direction, \(\Delta G=RT\ln(Q/K)\)

In Module 8 you will meet \(\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q\) and \(\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K\). Combine them and you get \(\Delta G=RT\ln\dfrac{Q}{K}\). When \(Q<K\) the logarithm is negative, so \(\Delta G<0\) and the forward reaction is spontaneous. When \(Q>K\), \(\Delta G>0\) and the backward reaction is spontaneous. So the Q-versus-K rule is just the sign of \(\Delta G\).

3.4 Worked examples

Example 1. For \(\mathrm{NOBr(g)\rightleftharpoons NO(g)+\tfrac12 Br_2(g)}\), \(K_p=0.15\) at a certain temperature. A mixture has \(p_{\mathrm{NOBr}}=0.80\) atm, \(p_{\mathrm{NO}}=0.30\) atm and \(p_{\mathrm{Br_2}}=0.10\) atm. Will \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) be formed or used up?

  1. Write Q with the same exponents as K. The coefficient \(\tfrac12\) becomes a power of \(\tfrac12\): \(Q_p=\dfrac{p_{\mathrm{NO}}\;p_{\mathrm{Br_2}}^{1/2}}{p_{\mathrm{NOBr}}}\).
  2. Substitute: \(Q_p=\dfrac{0.30\times(0.10)^{1/2}}{0.80}=\dfrac{0.30\times0.3162}{0.80}\approx0.119\).
  3. Compare: \(Q_p=0.119<K_p=0.15\).
  4. Conclusion: the reaction goes forward, so \(\mathrm{Br_2}\) is formed until \(Q_p\) reaches 0.15.

Example 2. For \(\mathrm{H_2(g)+I_2(g)\rightleftharpoons 2HI(g)}\), \(K_c=50\) at 700 K. A 1.0 L flask holds 0.50 mol \(\mathrm{H_2}\), 0.50 mol \(\mathrm{I_2}\) and 3.0 mol HI. Which way does it go?

  1. Concentrations (V = 1 L): \([\mathrm{H_2}]=[\mathrm{I_2}]=0.50\) M, \([\mathrm{HI}]=3.0\) M.
  2. \(Q_c=\dfrac{[\mathrm{HI}]^2}{[\mathrm{H_2}][\mathrm{I_2}]}=\dfrac{3.0^2}{0.50\times0.50}=\dfrac{9.0}{0.25}=36\).
  3. \(Q_c=36<K_c=50\), so the reaction goes forward: more HI forms at the expense of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and \(\mathrm{I_2}\).

Example 3. For \(\mathrm{A\rightleftharpoons B}\), \(K=9\). Starting with pure A, what percentage of A is converted at equilibrium?

  1. Let the fraction converted be \(f\). Then \([B]=f\) and \([A]=1-f\) (per unit starting amount).
  2. \(K=\dfrac{f}{1-f}=9\Rightarrow f=9-9f\Rightarrow 10f=9\Rightarrow f=0.9\).
  3. So 90% of A is converted. This agrees with \(K/(1+K)=9/10\). Since \(K\) is between \(10^{-3}\) and \(10^{3}\), a significant amount of A (10%) is still left.

3.5 Common traps

  • Mixing up the direction. Always say it as "Q < K → forward". Write it down before the calculation.
  • Using exponents of the wrong equation. If the equation is written with \(\tfrac12\) coefficients, Q and K both use powers of \(\tfrac12\).
  • Using K for a different temperature. K belongs to one temperature only.
  • Thinking a large K means fast. K says nothing about rate.
  • Including solids in Q. Leave them out, as in K.

3.6 Check yourself

Module 3 recap
  • Q has the form of K but uses present concentrations or pressures.
  • Q < K → forward; Q > K → backward; Q = K → equilibrium.
  • \(K>10^3\): nearly complete. \(K<10^{-3}\): hardly proceeds. In between: a mixture.
  • K gives extent, not speed.

4.3 Temperature and the van't Hoff equation

Heat can be treated like a reactant (in an endothermic reaction) or a product (in an exothermic one). Adding heat favours the heat-absorbing direction, so K responds:

Van't Hoff equation \[ \log\frac{K_2}{K_1}=\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{2.303\,R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2}\right) \] Use \(R=8.314\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}\) with \(\Delta H^\circ\) in joules per mole, and \(T\) in kelvin. Substitute \(\Delta H\) with its sign.
  • Endothermic (\(\Delta H>0\)): K increases when T increases.
  • Exothermic (\(\Delta H<0\)): K decreases when T increases.

Check with the sign: if \(T_2>T_1\) then \(\tfrac1{T_1}-\tfrac1{T_2}>0\), so for \(\Delta H>0\) the right-hand side is positive, \(K_2>K_1\). ✓

ADVANCED EXTRA: reading \(\Delta H^\circ\) and \(\Delta S^\circ\) from a graph

Combining \(\Delta G^\circ=-2.303RT\log K\) with \(\Delta G^\circ=\Delta H^\circ-T\Delta S^\circ\) (Module 8) gives \[ \log K=-\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{2.303R}\cdot\frac1T+\frac{\Delta S^\circ}{2.303R} \] This is a straight line, \(y=mx+c\), for \(y=\log K\) against \(x=1/T\). So slope \(=-\Delta H^\circ/(2.303R)\) and intercept \(=\Delta S^\circ/(2.303R)\). A line that falls with \(1/T\) (negative slope) means \(\Delta H^\circ>0\). Always keep the minus sign when you convert a slope to \(\Delta H^\circ\).

4.4 Worked examples

Example 1. The equilibrium constant for \(\mathrm{N_2+3H_2\rightleftharpoons 2NH_3}\) is 36 at some temperature. Find K for \(\mathrm{NH_3\rightleftharpoons \tfrac12 N_2+\tfrac32 H_2}\).

  1. Compare the new equation with the old one. It is reversed and the coefficients are halved.
  2. Reversing gives \(1/36\).
  3. Halving the coefficients raises to the power \(\tfrac12\): \(K_{new}=\left(\dfrac1{36}\right)^{1/2}=\dfrac16\approx0.167\).

Example 2. For a reaction with \(\Delta H^\circ=+40\) kJ/mol and \(K=1.0\times10^{-4}\) at 300 K, find K at 400 K.

  1. Convert: \(\Delta H^\circ=40\,000\) J/mol. \(2.303\times8.314=19.15\).
  2. \(\dfrac{1}{T_1}-\dfrac{1}{T_2}=\dfrac1{300}-\dfrac1{400}=0.003333-0.002500=8.333\times10^{-4}\).
  3. \(\log\dfrac{K_2}{K_1}=\dfrac{40\,000}{19.15}\times8.333\times10^{-4}=2089\times8.333\times10^{-4}\approx1.74\).
  4. \(\dfrac{K_2}{K_1}=10^{1.74}\approx55\), so \(K_2\approx55\times10^{-4}=5.5\times10^{-3}\).
  5. Sense check: endothermic reaction, higher T, so K rose. ✓

Example 3. \(\mathrm{A\rightleftharpoons B}\) has \(K_1=2\), \(\mathrm{B\rightleftharpoons C}\) has \(K_2=5\), all at the same temperature. Find K for (a) \(\mathrm{A\rightleftharpoons C}\), (b) \(\mathrm{\tfrac12A\rightleftharpoons \tfrac12C}\), (c) \(\mathrm{C\rightleftharpoons A}\).

  1. (a) Adding the two equations: \(K=K_1K_2=2\times5=10\).
  2. (b) Halving the coefficients: \(10^{1/2}=\sqrt{10}\approx3.16\).
  3. (c) Reversing (a): \(1/10=0.1\).

4.5 Common traps

  • Quoting a K without its equation. A K value is meaningless unless you know how the equation was written.
  • Saying pressure or a catalyst changes K. Only temperature does. They may change the position (pressure) or the speed (catalyst), never K.
  • Using kJ with R in joules. Convert \(\Delta H\) to J, or use \(R=0.008314\) kJ/mol/K.
  • Dropping the sign of \(\Delta H\). Substitute it with its sign.
  • Using Celsius. The van't Hoff equation needs kelvin.

4.6 Check yourself

Module 4 recap
  • K depends only on temperature (and on how the equation is written).
  • Reverse: \(1/K\). Multiply by \(n\): \(K^n\). Add: \(K_1K_2\). Subtract: \(K_1/K_2\).
  • \(\log\dfrac{K_2}{K_1}=\dfrac{\Delta H^\circ}{2.303R}\left(\dfrac1{T_1}-\dfrac1{T_2}\right)\).
  • Endothermic: K rises with T. Exothermic: K falls with T.
Module 5

Solving equilibrium problems with ICE tables

A reliable method for any "find the equilibrium composition" question.

After this module you can:
  • set up an ICE (Initial, Change, Equilibrium) table for gas and liquid systems
  • solve for the unknown using a perfect square, a quadratic, or a safe approximation
  • work in either concentrations or partial pressures and convert correctly

5.1 The method in five steps

  1. Write the balanced equation and the expression for K (leave out solids and pure liquids).
  2. Draw the ICE table. Row I: starting amounts. Row C: change in terms of \(x\), with signs. Row E: I + C.
  3. Link the changes by the coefficients. If the reactant with coefficient 1 loses \(x\), a reactant with coefficient 3 loses \(3x\), and a product with coefficient 2 gains \(2x\).
  4. Substitute the E row into K and solve for \(x\).
  5. Check: \(x\) must keep every amount positive (reject the impossible root), and putting the answer back must reproduce K.
Moles vs concentration Kc needs concentrations. If you work in moles, divide by the volume \(V\) first. When \(\Delta n_g=0\) the volume cancels and you may use moles directly. For Kp use partial pressures in the E row.

5.2 Three ways to solve for x

  • Keeping the root that makes an amount negative. Reject it.
  • Using the shortcut when K is not small. Check that \(x\) is under about 5% of the starting amount.
  • Mixing units. Do not put partial pressures into a \(K_c\) expression.

Module 6

Heterogeneous equilibria, vapour pressure and humidity

Equilibria with solids and liquids, and why a hydrate "dries out" or "soaks up" moisture.

After this module you can:
  • write Kc and Kp for equilibria that include solids and pure liquids
  • find Kp from the total pressure of a decomposition equilibrium
  • define vapour pressure and relative humidity
  • decide whether a hydrate will lose or gain water in air of a given humidity

6.1 Pure solids and liquids drop out

The "concentration" of a pure solid or liquid is just its density divided by its molar mass. That is a fixed number, so it cannot change during the reaction. Chemists fold it into K, which is the same as treating its active mass as 1.

Two consequences
  • Adding more solid changes nothing at equilibrium, as long as some of each solid is present. For \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\) decomposition in a closed vessel, \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) at a given temperature is fixed, whether you used 1 g or 100 g of limestone.
  • The solid must actually be there. If it is all used up, there is no equilibrium and the K relation does not apply.

6.2 Vapour pressure and relative humidity

Vapour pressure is the pressure of vapour in equilibrium with its liquid. For water, \(\mathrm{H_2O(l)\rightleftharpoons H_2O(g)}\) has \(K_p=p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}\), so the vapour pressure is a constant at a given temperature. It does not depend on how much liquid there is or on the volume. At 25 °C the vapour pressure of water is about 24 mm Hg (also called aqueous tension).

Relative humidity \[ \text{RH}=\frac{\text{actual partial pressure of water vapour in the air}}{\text{vapour pressure of water at that temperature}}\times100\% \] RH = 100% means the air is saturated: \(Q=K\) for \(\mathrm{H_2O(l)\rightleftharpoons H_2O(g)}\), so liquid water and vapour are in equilibrium.

6.3 Hydrates, drying and soaking up water

A salt hydrate can lose water vapour in a reversible reaction, for example \(\mathrm{MX\cdot2H_2O(s)\rightleftharpoons MX(s)+2H_2O(g)}\). Both solids drop out, so \(K_p=p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}^{\,2}\), and at a given temperature the hydrate and its lower form are in equilibrium only at one fixed water-vapour pressure: \(p_{eq}=K_p^{1/2}\).

Compare the water-vapour pressure in the air with \(p_{eq}\) (this is just Q against K):

  • Air \(p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}<p_{eq}\) (dry air): \(Q<K\), the reaction goes forward and the hydrate loses water. It "effloresces" (turns powdery).
  • Air \(p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}>p_{eq}\) (humid air): \(Q>K\), the reaction goes backward and the lower form absorbs water (hygroscopic).
  • Equal: equilibrium; no net change.

ADVANCED EXTRA: Henry's law (gas ⇌ solution)

The equilibrium \(\mathrm{gas\rightleftharpoons dissolved\ gas}\) obeys Henry's law: at constant temperature, the mole fraction of a gas dissolved in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid, \(p=K_H\,x\). Double the pressure and you double the dissolved gas. That is why a soda fizzes when you open the bottle: the pressure above it drops and the equilibrium shifts towards the gas. It is part of the JEE Main syllabus under gas–liquid equilibria.

6.4 Worked examples

Example 1. Solid \(\mathrm{NH_4HS}\) is heated in an evacuated vessel and decomposes by \(\mathrm{NH_4HS(s)\rightleftharpoons NH_3(g)+H_2S(g)}\). At equilibrium the total pressure is 0.80 atm. Find \(K_p\). Then find the total pressure if the vessel is changed so that \(p_{\mathrm{NH_3}}=0.80\) atm at the same temperature.

  1. The only gases come from the solid, in a 1 : 1 ratio, so each has half the total pressure: \(p_{\mathrm{NH_3}}=p_{\mathrm{H_2S}}=0.40\) atm.
  2. \(K_p=p_{\mathrm{NH_3}}\,p_{\mathrm{H_2S}}=0.40\times0.40=\mathbf{0.16\ atm^2}\).
  3. Second case: the temperature is the same, so \(K_p\) is still 0.16. With \(p_{\mathrm{NH_3}}=0.80\): \(p_{\mathrm{H_2S}}=\dfrac{0.16}{0.80}=0.20\) atm.
  4. Total pressure \(=0.80+0.20=1.00\) atm. Extra NH3 pushes the equilibrium backward and cuts down H2S.

Example 2. For the hypothetical hydrate \(\mathrm{MX\cdot2H_2O(s)\rightleftharpoons MX(s)+2H_2O(g)}\), \(K_p=4.0\times10^{-4}\) atm\(^2\) at 25 °C. At what relative humidity will the hydrate start to lose water? (Vapour pressure of water at 25 °C: 24 mm Hg.)

  1. \(K_p=p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}^{\,2}\), so \(p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}=\sqrt{4.0\times10^{-4}}=0.020\) atm.
  2. Convert: \(0.020\times760=15.2\) mm Hg.
  3. \(\text{RH}=\dfrac{15.2}{24}\times100=63.3\%\).
  4. Conclusion: if RH < 63%, the air's water pressure is below 15.2 mm Hg, \(Q<K\), and the hydrate loses water. Above 63% it stays as a hydrate (or absorbs more water).

Example 3. At a certain temperature, \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)\rightleftharpoons CaO(s)+CO_2(g)}\) has \(K_p=0.0100\) atm. Limestone is heated in open air (total pressure 1 atm) that contains 0.04% \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) by volume. Will it decompose?

  1. \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}=0.0004\times1\ \text{atm}=4\times10^{-4}\) atm.
  2. \(Q_p=p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}=4\times10^{-4}\) atm, which is less than \(K_p=0.0100\) atm.
  3. \(Q<K\), so the reaction goes forward: the limestone decomposes, and since the air is open, the \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) escapes and it keeps going until all \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\) is used up.
  4. Contrast: in a sealed vessel filled with \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) at 0.020 atm, \(Q>K\), so no decomposition occurs.

6.5 Common traps

  • Putting solids into K or into \(\Delta n_g\).
  • Thinking more solid raises the pressure. It does not; \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) depends only on temperature.
  • Forgetting the 1 : 1 split of total pressure in \(\mathrm{NH_4HS}\), where each gas has half. (If the gases are produced in another ratio, the split changes.)
  • Calling an open-vessel decomposition an equilibrium. It runs to completion.
  • Mixing mm Hg and atm in Kp and RH problems.

6.6 Check yourself

Module 6 recap
  • Pure solids and liquids are left out of K; their amount does not matter if some remains.
  • \(\mathrm{H_2O(l)\rightleftharpoons H_2O(g)}\): \(K_p\) = vapour pressure, fixed at a given temperature.
  • RH = (actual p(H₂O) / vapour pressure) × 100.
  • Hydrate: compare air's p(H₂O) with \(p_{eq}\). Lower → loses water; higher → gains water.

Degree of dissociation and vapour density

How to turn "fraction dissociated" into K, and use gas density to measure it.

After this module you can:
  • define the degree of dissociation \(\alpha\) and set up an ICE table with it
  • derive \(K_p\) in terms of \(\alpha\) and the total pressure
  • relate observed molar mass and vapour density to \(\alpha\)
  • explain why \(\alpha\) falls when pressure rises

7.1 What is \(\alpha\)?

The degree of dissociation \(\alpha\) is the fraction of the substance that has dissociated, defined per mole:

\[ \alpha=\frac{\text{moles dissociated}}{\text{initial moles}},\qquad \%\text{ dissociation}=\alpha\times100 \]

If 5 mol of \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) is taken and 2 mol dissociate, \(\alpha=2/5=0.4\), i.e. 40%.

General case: \(\mathrm{A_n(g)\rightleftharpoons nA(g)}\), starting with \(a\) mol and \(\alpha\) dissociated:

\(\mathrm{A_n}\)\(\mathrm{A}\)Total moles
Start\(a\)0\(a\)
Equilibrium\(a(1-\alpha)\)\(n\,a\alpha\)\(a\,[1+(n-1)\alpha]\)

Here \(n\) is the ratio of (sum of product coefficients) to (sum of reactant coefficients) when written per mole of the dissociating substance:

  • \(\mathrm{PCl_5\rightleftharpoons PCl_3+Cl_2}\): \(n=2\).
  • \(\mathrm{N_2O_4\rightleftharpoons 2NO_2}\): \(n=2\).
  • \(\mathrm{2NH_3\rightleftharpoons N_2+3H_2}\): per mole of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), \(n=\tfrac12+\tfrac32=2\).
  • \(\mathrm{2HI\rightleftharpoons H_2+I_2}\): \(n=1\). (No change in moles, so this method cannot be used.)

7.2 \(K_p\) in terms of \(\alpha\) (for \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\))

Start with 1 mol \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) and total pressure \(P\). At equilibrium: \(\mathrm{PCl_5}=1-\alpha\), \(\mathrm{PCl_3}=\mathrm{Cl_2}=\alpha\), total \(=1+\alpha\).

\[ p_{\mathrm{PCl_5}}=\frac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}P,\quad p_{\mathrm{PCl_3}}=p_{\mathrm{Cl_2}}=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}P \] \[ K_p=\frac{\left(\dfrac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}P\right)^2}{\dfrac{1-\alpha}{1+\alpha}P}=\frac{\alpha^2}{(1-\alpha)(1+\alpha)}P=\boxed{\frac{\alpha^2P}{1-\alpha^2}} \]

Rearranged: \(\alpha=\sqrt{\dfrac{K_p}{K_p+P}}\). As \(P\) increases, \(\alpha\) decreases. This is Le Chatelier at work: higher pressure favours the side with fewer moles (\(\mathrm{PCl_5}\)).

7.3 Observed molar mass and vapour density

The mass of gas is conserved, but the number of moles rises as the gas dissociates. So the average molar mass of the mixture falls, and so does the gas density (at fixed T and P).

Key relations (for \(\mathrm{A_n\rightleftharpoons nA}\)) \[ M_{obs}=\frac{M_{th}}{1+(n-1)\alpha},\qquad \frac{D}{d}=1+(n-1)\alpha,\qquad \alpha=\frac{D-d}{(n-1)\,d}=\frac{M_{th}-M_{obs}}{(n-1)M_{obs}} \] \(D\) = vapour density without dissociation \(=M_{th}/2\); \(d\) = observed vapour density of the mixture \(=M_{obs}/2\). (Vapour density = density of gas ÷ density of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) under the same conditions, so it is half the molar mass.)

A good way to remember: moles up, density down. The ratio \(D/d\) is simply the ratio of final moles to initial moles.

7.4 Worked examples

Example 1. The observed vapour density of a mixture formed from \(\mathrm{N_2O_4\rightleftharpoons 2NO_2}\) is 34.5. Find \(\alpha\), and the moles of \(\mathrm{NO_2}\) formed from 0.50 mol (46 g) of \(\mathrm{N_2O_4}\).

  1. \(M_{th}=92\), so \(D=92/2=46\). Here \(n=2\).
  2. \(\alpha=\dfrac{D-d}{(n-1)d}=\dfrac{46-34.5}{34.5}=\dfrac{11.5}{34.5}=\dfrac13\approx0.333\).
  3. Moles of \(\mathrm{NO_2}=2a\alpha=2\times0.50\times\tfrac13=0.333\) mol; moles of \(\mathrm{N_2O_4}\) left \(=0.50\times\tfrac23=0.333\) mol.
  4. Check: total \(=0.667=0.50\times(1+\tfrac13)\). ✓

Example 2. \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) is 40% dissociated at a total pressure of 2.0 atm. Find \(K_p\).

  1. \(\alpha=0.40\), so \(\alpha^2=0.16\).
  2. \(K_p=\dfrac{\alpha^2P}{1-\alpha^2}=\dfrac{0.16\times2.0}{1-0.16}=\dfrac{0.32}{0.84}=0.381\) atm.
  3. Check via pressures: \(p_{\mathrm{PCl_3}}=p_{\mathrm{Cl_2}}=\tfrac{0.4}{1.4}\times2=0.571\), \(p_{\mathrm{PCl_5}}=\tfrac{0.6}{1.4}\times2=0.857\); \(K_p=\dfrac{0.571^2}{0.857}=0.381\). ✓

Example 3. For \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\), \(K_p=0.25\) atm. Find \(\alpha\) at a total pressure of 1.0 atm, and compare with 5.0 atm.

  1. Use \(\alpha=\sqrt{\dfrac{K_p}{K_p+P}}\).
  2. At 1.0 atm: \(\alpha=\sqrt{\dfrac{0.25}{1.25}}=\sqrt{0.2}=0.447\) (44.7%).
  3. At 5.0 atm: \(\alpha=\sqrt{\dfrac{0.25}{5.25}}=\sqrt{0.0476}=0.218\) (21.8%).
  4. Higher pressure roughly halved the dissociation, as Le Chatelier predicts.

7.5 Common traps

  • Using the method for \(n=1\). \(\mathrm{2HI\rightleftharpoons H_2+I_2}\) has no change in moles, so density does not change and cannot give \(\alpha\).
  • Mixing up D and d. \(D\) is the undissociated value (larger), \(d\) is the observed one (smaller). \(D/d\ge1\).
  • Forgetting that \(\alpha\) is per mole of starting substance, not per mole of the mixture.
  • Using \(K_p=\alpha^2P/(1-\alpha^2)\) for other reactions. It is specific to \(n=2\) of the \(\mathrm{PCl_5}\) type.
  • Saying pressure increases \(\alpha\). For \(n>1\), it decreases \(\alpha\).

Module 8

Thermodynamics of equilibrium: \(\Delta G\), \(\Delta G^\circ\) and K

Why equilibrium happens at all, and how free energy links to K.

After this module you can:
  • explain why \(\Delta G=0\) at equilibrium but \(\Delta G^\circ\) is usually not
  • use \(\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K\) in both directions
  • use \(\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q\) to find the direction of change
  • combine \(\Delta H^\circ\), \(\Delta S^\circ\) and T to decide spontaneity

8.1 The driving force

Equilibrium is a compromise between two tendencies: systems like to lower their energy and they like to increase their entropy. Gibbs free energy combines both: \(G=H-TS\). A process happens spontaneously when it lowers G.

8.2 The two key equations

Away from equilibrium \[ \Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q=\Delta G^\circ+2.303\,RT\log Q \] At equilibrium \(\Delta G=0\) and \(Q=K\), so \[ \Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K=-2.303\,RT\log K \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad K=e^{-\Delta G^\circ/RT} \] Use \(R=8.314\ \mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}K^{-1}}\) and watch that \(\Delta G^\circ\) is in joules.

positive\(K<1\)reactants favoured at equilibrium
\(\Delta G\) and \(\Delta G^\circ\) are different things \(\Delta G^\circ\) is for all substances at standard state (1 atm for gases, 1 M for solutes, pure solids and liquids). It is a fixed number at a given T. \(\Delta G\) is for the actual mixture and changes as the reaction proceeds, reaching 0 at equilibrium.
A useful number at 298 K \(2.303RT=2.303\times8.314\times298\approx5.7\) kJ/mol. So every \(-5.7\) kJ in \(\Delta G^\circ\) multiplies K by 10: \(-5.7\to K\approx10\); \(-11.4\to K\approx100\); \(+5.7\to K\approx0.1\).

8.3 Why K has no units

Strictly, K is built from activities, which are unitless ratios to a standard state. Pure solids and liquids have activity 1; ideal gases use partial pressure (in atm); solutes use molarity. That is why Kc and Kp in the earlier modules were really "this number, with the units we agreed to use".

8.4 Spontaneity and temperature

\(\Delta G^\circ=\Delta H^\circ-T\Delta S^\circ\). Putting this with \(\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K\): \[ \ln K=-\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{RT}+\frac{\Delta S^\circ}{R} \] This is the van't Hoff relation of Module 4.

ADVANCED EXTRA: decomposition temperature of a solid

For \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)\rightleftharpoons CaO(s)+CO_2(g)}\), \(K_p=p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\). 8.5 Worked examples

Example 1. A reaction has \(\Delta G^\circ=-5.7\) kJ/mol at 298 K. Find K.

  1. \(\ln K=-\dfrac{\Delta G^\circ}{RT}=\dfrac{5700}{8.314\times298}=\dfrac{5700}{2477.6}=2.30\).
  2. \(K=e^{2.30}\approx10\).
  3. Negative \(\Delta G^\circ\) gave \(K>1\), as expected.

Example 2. A reaction has \(\Delta H^\circ=+60\) kJ/mol and \(\Delta S^\circ=+200\) J/mol/K. Find the temperature above which it is spontaneous, and K at 500 K.

  1. Switch point: \(T=\dfrac{\Delta H^\circ}{\Delta S^\circ}=\dfrac{60\,000}{200}=300\) K. Both signs are positive, so it is spontaneous above 300 K.
  2. At 500 K: \(\Delta G^\circ=60\,000-500\times200=-40\,000\) J/mol.
  3. \(\ln K=\dfrac{40\,000}{8.314\times500}=\dfrac{40\,000}{4157}=9.62\).
  4. \(K=e^{9.62}\approx1.5\times10^{4}\). Strongly product-favoured.

Example 3. At 300 K, a reaction has \(\Delta G^\circ=-10\) kJ/mol. The mixture has \(Q=100\). Which way does it go?

  1. \(RT=8.314\times300=2494\) J/mol. \(\ln Q=\ln100=4.605\).
  2. \(\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q=-10\,000+2494\times4.605=-10\,000+11\,486=+1486\) J/mol.
  3. \(\Delta G>0\), so the forward reaction is non-spontaneous: the mixture goes backward.
  4. Cross-check with K: \(K=e^{10\,000/2494}=e^{4.01}\approx55\). \(Q=100>K=55\), so backward. ✓

8.6 Common traps

  • Saying \(\Delta G^\circ=0\) at equilibrium. It is \(\Delta G\) that is zero. \(\Delta G^\circ\) equals zero only when \(K=1\).
  • Mixing kJ and J. \(R\) is in J, so convert \(\Delta G^\circ\) to joules.
  • Thinking negative \(\Delta G^\circ\) means a fast reaction. It says nothing about rate.
  • Using \(\ln\) with a log formula. \(\ln x=2.303\log x\). Keep them consistent.
  • Dropping a sign when substituting \(\Delta H^\circ\) and \(\Delta S^\circ\).

Predicting how an equilibrium responds when you disturb it.

After this module you can:
  • state Le Chatelier's principle and justify each prediction using Q and K
  • predict the effect of concentration, volume, pressure, inert gas, temperature and catalyst
  • distinguish between changes that shift the position and the one that changes K
  • choose sensible conditions for the Haber, contact and Birkeland–Eyde processes

9.1 The principle

Le Chatelier's principle If a change is applied to a system at equilibrium, the system shifts in the direction that tends to reduce the effect of the change, and a new equilibrium is established.

Think of a crowded lift: if more people squeeze in at one side, the crowd re-spreads to relieve the pressure. The principle tells you the direction of the shift. Behind every prediction is the Q-vs-K rule from Module 3: the change makes \(Q\ne K\), and the reaction moves to restore \(Q=K\).

9.6 Worked examples

Example 1. \(\mathrm{PCl_5(g)\rightleftharpoons PCl_3(g)+Cl_2(g)}\), \(\Delta H>0\), is at equilibrium. Predict the effect of: (a) adding \(\mathrm{Cl_2}\); (b) halving the volume; (c) adding argon at constant volume; (d) adding argon at constant pressure; (e) cooling.

  1. (a) Add a product: \(Q\) rises above K → backward.
  2. (b) \(\Delta n_g=+1\); halving V raises pressure → shift to fewer moles → backward.
  3. (c) Concentrations unchanged → no shift.
  4. (d) Volume must increase to hold P, like a pressure drop → shift to more moles → forward.
  5. (e) Endothermic, so cooling favours the exothermic (backward) direction → backward, and K decreases.

Example 2. For \(\mathrm{N_2+3H_2\rightleftharpoons 2NH_3}\), \(\Delta H=-92\) kJ, which conditions give the best equilibrium yield of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\), and why is the real plant temperature not the lowest possible?

  1. \(\Delta n_g=-2\): high pressure shifts forward.
  2. Exothermic: low temperature shifts forward and raises K.
  3. But at low temperature, molecules react very slowly and the equilibrium would take far too long to establish. A catalyst (iron) is used, with a moderate temperature as a compromise between yield and rate.
  4. Removing \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) by liquefaction as it forms also pulls the equilibrium forward.

Example 3. \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)\rightleftharpoons CaO(s)+CO_2(g)}\) at equilibrium in a closed vessel. What happens to \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) when (a) more \(\mathrm{CaO}\) is added, (b) the volume is doubled, (c) the temperature is raised?

  1. (a) \(K_p=p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) and solids are left out. No shift, so \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) is unchanged.
  2. (b) Doubling V makes \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) momentarily fall below K. The reaction shifts forward, more \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\) decomposes, and \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) returns to the same value (K has not changed). More moles of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) are present, but the pressure is the same. This assumes enough \(\mathrm{CaCO_3}\) remains.
  3. (c) Endothermic, so K and \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) both increase.

9.7 Common traps

  • Adding inert gas always shifts the equilibrium. Not at constant volume.
  • A catalyst raises the yield. It only speeds up arrival at the same equilibrium.
  • Pressure always matters. If \(\Delta n_g=0\) it has no effect on position.
  • Pressure or concentration changes K. They do not. Only temperature does.
  • Adding a solid shifts the equilibrium. It does not.
  • Forgetting rate in industry. Best yield and best rate often need different temperatures.

  • Pressure/volume: shift to fewer moles on compression, more moles on expansion; nothing if \(\Delta n_g=0\).
  • Inert gas: no effect at constant V; acts like an expansion at constant P.
  • Temperature is the only factor that changes K. A catalyst changes only the rate.
Module 10

Simultaneous equilibria

When two equilibria share a species.

After this module you can:
  • recognise a system with two or more coupled equilibria
  • build an ICE table that tracks the shared species through both equilibria
  • solve for the equilibrium constants or composition

10.1 The one rule

Total concentration of the common species If two or more equilibria exist in the same container and share a species, then in every equilibrium expression the concentration (or pressure) of that shared species is its total amount at equilibrium, from all the reactions together.

Why? There is only one container. The gas does not "know" which reaction produced it. So whichever K you write, you must use the actual concentration in the vessel.

Example: \(\mathrm{A(s)\rightleftharpoons X(g)+Y(g)}\) and \(\mathrm{B(s)\rightleftharpoons Z(g)+Y(g)}\). Let the first produce \(t\) mol of X and Y, and the second \(u\) mol of Z and Y. The total Y is \(t+u\). So \(K_1=[X][Y]=t(t+u)\) and \(K_2=[Z][Y]=u(t+u)\) (in suitable units).

10.2 Method

  1. Write both equilibria and both K expressions.
  2. Choose one unknown per reaction (the amount reacted in each, e.g. \(t\) and \(u\), or \(a\) and \(b\)).
  3. Build the E row for each species. For the shared species, add the contributions from both reactions.
  4. Substitute into both K expressions and solve the simultaneous equations. A ratio of the two K's often removes the common factor.

10.3 Lab: two solids sharing a gas

Take \(\mathrm{A(s)\rightleftharpoons X(g)+Y(g)}\) (\(K_{p1}\)) and \(\mathrm{B(s)\rightleftharpoons Z(g)+Y(g)}\) (\(K_{p2}\)). Let \(p_X=a\), \(p_Z=b\), so \(p_Y=a+b\). Then \(K_{p1}=a(a+b)\) and \(K_{p2}=b(a+b)\).

Partial pressures from two Kp values

Dividing the two K expressions gives \(a/b=K_{p1}/K_{p2}\). Adding them gives \((a+b)^2=K_{p1}+K_{p2}\), so \(p_Y=\sqrt{K_{p1}+K_{p2}}\). Check that the output obeys this.

10.4 Worked examples

Example 1. In the system above, \(K_{p1}=6\) atm² and \(K_{p2}=3\) atm². Find the equilibrium partial pressures and the total pressure.

  1. Let \(p_X=a\), \(p_Z=b\), \(p_Y=a+b\). Then \(a(a+b)=6\) and \(b(a+b)=3\).
  2. Divide: \(\dfrac ab=\dfrac63=2\), so \(a=2b\).
  3. Substitute: \(b(2b+b)=3b^2=3\Rightarrow b=1\) atm, so \(a=2\) atm and \(p_Y=3\) atm.
  4. Check: \(2\times3=6\) ✓, \(1\times3=3\) ✓. Total pressure \(=2+1+3=6\) atm.

Example 2. 2.0 mol of solid \(\mathrm{NH_4HS}\) is heated in a 4.0 L evacuated flask. Two equilibria exist: \(\mathrm{NH_4HS(s)\rightleftharpoons NH_3(g)+H_2S(g)}\) (\(K_{c1}\)) and \(\mathrm{NH_3(g)\rightleftharpoons \tfrac12N_2(g)+\tfrac32H_2(g)}\) (\(K_{c2}\)). Of the solid, 1.0 mol has decomposed in the first equilibrium (1.0 mol is still left). 0.30 mol of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) is found at equilibrium. Find \(K_{c1}\) and \(K_{c2}\).

  1. First equilibrium makes 1.0 mol each of \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2S}\). Let \(x\) mol of the \(\mathrm{NH_3}\) then dissociate.
  2. The second gives \(\mathrm{N_2}=x/2\) and \(\mathrm{H_2}=3x/2\). Since \(\mathrm{H_2}=0.30\): \(3x/2=0.30\Rightarrow x=0.20\) mol.
  3. Moles at equilibrium: \(\mathrm{NH_3}=1.0-0.20=0.80\) (total after both reactions), \(\mathrm{H_2S}=1.0\), \(\mathrm{N_2}=0.10\), \(\mathrm{H_2}=0.30\).
  4. Concentrations (÷ 4.0 L): \(\mathrm{NH_3}=0.20\), \(\mathrm{H_2S}=0.25\), \(\mathrm{N_2}=0.025\), \(\mathrm{H_2}=0.075\) M.
  5. \(K_{c1}=[\mathrm{NH_3}][\mathrm{H_2S}]=0.20\times0.25=\mathbf{0.050}\) (mol/L)².
  6. \(K_{c2}=\dfrac{[\mathrm{N_2}]^{1/2}[\mathrm{H_2}]^{3/2}}{[\mathrm{NH_3}]}=\dfrac{(0.025)^{1/2}(0.075)^{3/2}}{0.20}=\dfrac{0.1581\times0.02054}{0.20}\approx\mathbf{1.6\times10^{-2}}\) (mol/L).

10.5 Common traps

  • Using only one reaction's amount of the shared species in a K expression. Always use the total.
  • Forgetting the coefficient (e.g. \(\mathrm{H_2}=3x/2\), not \(x\)).
  • Using initial moles of the solid for the first equilibrium. It is the amount that has decomposed that matters.
  • Dividing by V twice or not at all when the reaction changes the number of moles.
