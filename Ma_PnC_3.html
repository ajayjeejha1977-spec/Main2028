Permutations & Combinations – Remember Sheet

For each weak area: how to think, the methods and tricks, and the mistakes students usually make with the way to avoid each.

Before any problem: the 6-question routine

  1. Are the objects distinct or identical? Are the groups/positions labelled or identical?
  2. Does order matter? (arrangement, rank, position \(\Rightarrow\) yes; committee, team, subset \(\Rightarrow\) no)
  3. Does the source have repeats (letters, digits, balls)? Is repetition allowed in the answer?
  4. Underline the words: at least, at most, exactly, between, greater than, not together. Is the end-point included?
  5. Fill the most restricted place first (first digit \(\ne0\), last digit for divisibility, fixed persons).
  6. Choose direct or complement, then test the method on a small case (2 or 3 objects) before trusting it.

1 · Repeated letters & digits

How to think. Two stages: first decide which letters you use (a pattern of repeats), then arrange them: \(\dfrac{k!}{\prod r_i!}\). Never arrange before you know the pattern.

Methods & tricks

  1. List the letters with their counts first (BARRACK: A×2, R×2, B, C, K).
  2. \(k\)-letter words: split into patterns. For 4 letters: all distinct; one pair + 2 distinct \(=\#\text{pair letters}\cdot\binom{d}{2}\cdot\frac{4!}{2!}\); two pairs \(=\binom{p}{2}\cdot\frac{4!}{2!2!}\); a triple + 1 if a letter occurs 3 times.
  3. Vowel/consonant conditions: do vowels and consonants separately by pattern, then multiply and arrange (DEVASTATION: 1440 + 120 + 72 + 6 = 1638).
  4. "At most 2 different digits" (4-digit): one digit \(9\); two non-zero digits \(\binom92(2^4-2)\); digits \(\{0,a\}\): \(9\,(2^3-1)\). Total 576.
  5. Dictionary rank: sort the letters; for each position count the kinds of smaller letters left, multiply by the arrangements of the rest (divide by repeats), add all, then add 1.
  6. For many colours with limits (balls), use the generating function \(\prod\sum_{k\le c_i}x^k/k!\) and read \(n!\,[x^n]\).

Common mistakes \(\to\) how to avoid

  1. Treating repeated letters as different (using \(n!\) or \(^{n}P_r\)). Fix: write counts first; ask "are these two letters really interchangeable?"
  2. Missing a pattern (usually the two-pairs case). Fix: list patterns on paper and tick each one.
  3. Dividing by \(2!\) in a case with no pair. Fix: divide only by the repeats that actually occur in that pattern.
  4. In rank: counting a repeated smaller letter twice, or forgetting the final \(+1\). Fix: count each kind once; "words before + 1".

2 · Divisibility & "greater than N"

How to think. A divisibility rule turns the question into a condition on digits. Write the conditions as a list (digit set, first digit, last digit, sum rule) and make sure every item is used.

Methods & tricks

  1. \(\div2\): last digit even. \(\div5\): last digit 0 or 5. \(\div4\): last two digits. \(\div3\) or \(9\): digit sum. \(\div11\): (sum of odd places) \(-\) (sum of even places) is a multiple of 11.
  2. Omission method (\(\div3\)): if you use \(k\) of \(n\) digits, find which digits to omit so the remaining sum is a multiple of 3 (0,1,2,3,4 sum 10: omit 1 or 4).
  3. \(\div11\) with six distinct digits and sum 24: the difference must be even and a multiple of 11, so it is \(0\): both place-groups sum to 12. Split the digits into two triples, arrange each, then subtract numbers with a leading 0.
  4. "Greater than \(N\)": break by prefix. Bigger first digit; same first digit and bigger second; ... down to the tail. (\(>4321\) with digits 0–5: \(216+72+18+4=310\).)
  5. Interval \([a,b]\): count from \(a\) up to the next round number, whole blocks, then the last partial block.

Common mistakes \(\to\) how to avoid

  1. Allowing a leading 0. Fix: choose the first digit first, always.
  2. Using the last digit for \(\div4\), or digit sum for \(\div11\). Fix: write the rule beside the question before starting.
  3. Ignoring one end of an interval. Fix: test the smallest and largest allowed number in your count.
  4. Counting \(N\) itself when the question says strictly greater. Fix: circle "strictly" or "at least".

3 · Inclusion–exclusion & sets

How to think. Translate the words into sets: "at least one" is a union, "none" is the complement. Draw a Venn diagram before you calculate.

Methods & tricks

  1. \(n(A\cup B\cup C)=\sum n(A)-\sum n(A\cap B)+n(A\cap B\cap C)\); none \(=\text{total}-n(A\cup B\cup C)\).
  2. Multiples: \(n(A\cap B)=\lfloor N/\mathrm{lcm}\rfloor\), not a product. (140 students, 2, 3, 5: \(70+46+28-23-14-9+4=102\), none \(=38\).)
  3. Sums work the same way: add the arithmetic series for each set, subtract overlaps (HCF(91,n)>1: \(2107+1196-182=3121\)).
  4. "No two alike together": total \(-\) glue each alike pair, with inclusion–exclusion on the pairs (KUTKUT: \(90-60=30\)).
  5. Derangements: \(D_4=9,\ D_5=44\). If card 1 is forced into envelope 2: \(D_4+D_5=53\).
  6. Disjoint subsets of an \(n\)-set: ordered pairs \(3^n\); unordered \(=\frac{3^n+1}2\).

Common mistakes \(\to\) how to avoid

  1. Dropping the triple overlap or its sign. Fix: write the formula skeleton (+, \(-\), +) first, then fill each term.
  2. Multiplying instead of using the lcm; counting the multiple of both numbers twice. Fix: list the overlap sets explicitly.
  3. Stopping at \(n(A\cup B\cup C)\) when the question asks for "none". Fix: underline what is asked.
  4. Counting ordered pairs when the question is unordered. Fix: test with a tiny set.

4 · Gaps, non-consecutive & recursion

How to think. Either "place the chosen items in the gaps between the others" or "what happens in the last step?" The second gives a recurrence.

Methods & tricks

  1. Choose \(r\) of \(n\) in a line with no two consecutive: \(\binom{n-r+1}{r}\). (4 visit-days in 15 days: \(\binom{12}{4}=495\).)
  2. Gap method for people/letters: arrange the free ones, make \(m+1\) gaps, then choose gaps (distinct items \(\Rightarrow\) P, identical items \(\Rightarrow\) C).
  3. Recurrence by the last step: stairs of 1 or 2 steps \(a_n=a_{n-1}+a_{n-2}\) (\(a_1=1,a_2=2\); 10 steps: 89). Strings with no "22": \(a_1=2,a_2=3\), same rule.
  4. "Both digits used": count all sequences and remove the single-digit ones (143 for ten digits).

Common mistakes \(\to\) how to avoid

  1. Wrong starting values. Fix: list the first 3 terms by hand and check the rule.
  2. Forgetting the end gaps (the "+1" in \(n-r+1\)). Fix: draw the line of ○ and mark every gap, including both ends.
  3. Using combinations when the order of steps matters. Fix: ask "does 1,2 differ from 2,1?"
  4. Forgetting to remove the excluded sequence (all 5s). Fix: re-read the condition at the end.

5 · Grouping & distribution

How to think. Decide first: are the groups labelled (different names, sizes, companies) or identical? Are the objects distinct? Do not pick a formula until both are known.

Methods & tricks

  1. Labelled groups of sizes \(a,b,c\): \(\frac{n!}{a!\,b!\,c!}\). Equal-sized unlabelled groups: also divide by (number of equal groups)!.
  2. When only limits are given (6 persons, 4 rooms, each 1–2), list the size patterns first: only (2,2,1,1). Then choose which rooms get 2, then distribute: \(\binom42\cdot\frac{6!}{2!2!}=1080\).
  3. Preferences: place the restricted people first, then the free ones (Bombay clerks: \(\binom52\cdot6\cdot90=5400\)).
  4. Forbidden placements: total \(-\) A \(-\) B \(+\) (A and B) (\(1260-280-420+105=665\)).
  5. Choosing from boxes with limits: a generating function per box, multiply, read the coefficient (21816).
  6. Two sources with equal totals: sum over the number taken from the first source; symmetric structure often gives a sum of squares (485).
  7. Identical + distinct mixture: choose \(k\) distinct, the identical ones fill the rest, sum over \(k\) (\(2^{20}\)).

Common mistakes \(\to\) how to avoid

  1. Not dividing by \((2!)^m\) for equal-sized groups, or dividing when groups are labelled. Fix: write "labelled" or "unlabelled" beside the question.
  2. Missing a size pattern. Fix: list all patterns that satisfy the limits and add them.
  3. Ordered versus unordered confusion. Fix: test with 4 objects.
  4. Handling restricted people last. Fix: always place them first.

6 · Words into algebra / number theory

How to think. Turn the sentence into an equation or a divisor count before counting anything. Then check the answer in the original sentence.

Methods & tricks

  1. Polygon: triangles \(T_n=\binom n3\); \(T_{n+1}-T_n=\binom n2\).
  2. Triangular numbers: \(\frac{n(n+1)}2\). Form the equation in \(n\), solve, reject negative or non-integer roots (side 19, balls 190).
  3. LCM pairs: for each prime with LCM exponent \(e\), the exponent pairs with maximum \(e\) number \(2e+1\); multiply over primes (\(5\cdot9\cdot5=225\)).
  4. Divisors of \(\prod p_i^{e_i}\): \(\prod(e_i+1)\); splitting into two factors: half of that (check the perfect-square case).
  5. Axis triangles with vertices \((a,0),(0,b)\) and area 50: \(|ab|=100\), count divisors and multiply by 4 sign choices (36).

Common mistakes \(\to\) how to avoid

  1. Using \(e+1\) instead of \(2e+1\) for LCM pairs. Fix: test with one prime and \(e=1\): the pairs are (0,1),(1,0),(1,1) = 3.
  2. Forgetting sign choices or accepting a negative root. Fix: re-read what kind of number \(n\) must be.
  3. Misreading the given equation. Fix: substitute your final value back into it.

7 · Circular arrangements & colourings

How to think. Rotations make arrangements equal, so fix one person and arrange the rest. Then apply any condition.

Methods & tricks

  1. \(n\) people at a round table: \((n-1)!\). Two persons adjacent: glue them, \((n-2)!\cdot2\). Not adjacent: \((n-1)!-2(n-2)!\) (8 persons: \(5040-1440=3600\)).
  2. Men and women alternating (\(n\) each): fix one man, \((n-1)!\,n!\).
  3. Colouring a cycle of \(n\) with \(k\) colours, neighbours different: \((k-1)^n+(-1)^n(k-1)\). For \(n=5,k=3\): \(32-2=30\). Test: \(n=3,k=3\) gives 6.

Common mistakes \(\to\) how to avoid

  1. Using \(n!\) for a round table. Fix: fix one person first.
  2. Using the straight-line count \(k(k-1)^{n-1}\) for a circle. Fix: the last person is also next to the first; use the cycle formula.
  3. Forgetting the factor 2 for the glued pair. Fix: ask "can the two swap places?"

Habits that remove most slips

  1. Write the plan in one line before calculating ("patterns of repeats, then arrange").
  2. Check the final number against the total count — a part cannot exceed the whole.
  3. After solving, try a 2–3 object version and count by listing.
  4. When your answer is not among the options, re-check the three usual causes: a missed case, a leading zero, a repeat not divided.
  5. After a test, tick the real cause for each wrong question; the Mistake summary will show which habit to fix first.
