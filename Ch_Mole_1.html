1 · Atoms, the mole & molar volume

The big idea. Atoms are far too small to weigh one by one, so we use relative masses (carbon-12 scale) and count in moles. One mole is 6.02 × 1023 entities, the number of atoms in exactly 12 g of C-12. The mole links three things: mass, number of particles and gas volume.

Formulas and facts to know cold

Atomic mass unit
1 amu = (1/12) × mass of one C-12 atom
= 1.66 × 10−24 g; also called 1 Dalton (u)
Relative atomic mass
mass of atom ÷ (1/12 mass of C-12 atom)
Numerically equals the number of nucleons (O = 16). Atomic mass = R.A.M. × 1 amu
Moles
n = w/M = N/NA = VSTP/22.4 L
M = molar mass in g/mol
Gram atomic / molecular mass
O = 16 g · O2 = 32 g
Mass of one mole of atoms / molecules
STP and molar volume
273 K, 1 atm → 22.4 L/mol
Measuring gas volume is counting molecules
Avogadro and Gay-Lussac
equal V, same T, P → equal molecules
Gases combine in simple volume ratios: H2 + Cl2 → 2HCl (1 : 1 : 2)

Dalton's atomic theory

PostulateMeaning
Indivisible atomsMatter is made of very small indivisible particles
Identical atomsAll atoms of one element are identical in mass, size and shape
ConservationAtoms are not created or destroyed in a chemical process
Different elementsAtoms of different elements differ
Memory hooks
  • The Y-map: put mole in the centre. × NA gives particles, × 22.4 L gives gas volume at STP, × molar mass gives mass. Go back by dividing.
  • 1 mol of anything = NA pieces. 16 g of O atoms is exactly NA atoms.
Traps and exceptions
  • Avogadro counts molecules, not atoms. 1 mol H2SO4 holds 2 mol H, 1 mol S and 4 mol O atoms.
  • amu is not grams. One atom of O is 16 amu; one mole of O atoms is 16 g.
  • Source typo: the PDF says "molecular mass is the mass of 1 atom"; it means 1 molecule.
Worked example: moles of each element in 294 g H2SO4 (M = 98)

294/98 = 3 mol H2SO4. Each molecule has 2 H, 1 S, 4 O, so 6 mol H, 3 mol S and 12 mol O.

2 · Average mass, % composition & formulas

The big idea. A real sample is a mixture of isotopes or gases, so we use weighted averages. Percentage composition turns a formula into masses; going the other way gives the empirical formula (simplest ratio), and the molar mass then fixes the molecular formula.

Formulas

Average atomic mass
Σ(aixi) / 100
a = isotopic mass, x = mole % (Cl: 75 × 35 + 25 × 37 → 35.5)
Mean molar mass
Σ niMi / Σ ni
Weights are moles (or mole %, which sum to 100)
Mass % of an element
(mass in 1 mol ÷ molar mass) × 100
NH3: N = 14/17 = 82.35 %, H = 17.65 %
Molecular formula
= n × empirical, n = M(mol) / M(emp)
C2H2 and C6H6 both have empirical CH
Relative density, vapour density
R.D. = M1/M2 · V.D. = M/2
V.D. of N2O5 = 108/2 = 54

Empirical formula table (C 40.687 %, H 5.085 %, O 54.228 %)

Element% ÷ at. mass÷ smallest× to make whole
C3.39012
H5.0851.53
O3.38912

Empirical formula C2H3O2 (mass 59); molecular mass 118 gives n = 2, so the molecular formula is C4H6O4.

Memory hooks
  • Divide, divide, multiply: ÷ atomic mass, ÷ smallest value, × 2 or 3 to clear fractions (1.5 → ×2; 1.33 → ×3; 2.5 → ×2).
Traps and exceptions
  • Use molecular masses for gases: in the polluted-air example O is taken as O2 = 32 and N as N2 = 28 (not 16 and 14): (16×32 + 80×28 + 3×44 + 1×64)/100 = 29.48.
  • Cl is 35.5, not 35. The average mass is the one used in all calculations and in the periodic table.
  • Do not round early: 1.5 is not 1; it means multiply everything by 2.
Worked example: formula of an oxide with N = 25.94 %, O = 74.06 %

25.94/14 = 1.85 and 74.06/16 = 4.63. Divide by 1.85: 1 and 2.5. Multiply by 2: 2 and 5. Empirical formula N2O5.

3 · Stoichiometry from a balanced equation

The big idea. The coefficients of a balanced equation are mole ratios. Mass–mass, mass–volume and mole–mole problems are all one idea: convert to moles, use the ratio, convert back.

Balanced equation checklist

AttributeMeaning
Atom balance (POAC)Same number of atoms of each element on both sides
Charge balanceCharge conserved on either side
StatesPhysical states shown in brackets
Molecular formsReagents written as molecules, not atoms
CoefficientsGive relative molar ratios

Three ways to read 2KClO3 → 2KCl + 3O2

Mass–mass
m(A)/(a·MA) = m(B)/(b·MB)
2 × 122.5 : 2 × 74.5 : 3 × 32
Mass–volume (STP)
m(A)/(a·MA) = V(B)/(b × 22.4 L)
2 × 122.5 g gives 3 × 22.4 L of O2
Mole–mole
nA/a = nB/b = nC/c = nD/d
For aA + bB → cC + dD
Memory hooks
  • Mole is the hub. Mass ÷ M → moles → × coefficient ratio → moles of the other species → × M or × 22.4.
Traps and exceptions
  • Balance first. An unbalanced equation gives wrong ratios.
  • Molecules are not conserved, atoms are. N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3: 4 molecules become 2, yet the mass stays 34 g.
  • 22.4 L is only for gases at STP. Never use it for solids or liquids.
Worked example: 367.5 g KClO3 (M = 122.5) is heated

367.5/122.5 = 3 mol. KCl = 3 mol = 223.5 g. O2 = 3 × 3/2 = 4.5 mol = 144 g, which is 4.5 × 22.4 = 100.8 L at STP.

4 · Limiting reagent

The big idea. The reactant that is used up first is the limiting reagent; it fixes how much product forms. The other reactant is in excess. When given moles are not in the ratio of the coefficients, one reactant must be limiting.

How to find it (3 steps)

StepAction
1Divide the given moles of each reactant by its coefficient
2The smallest quotient marks the limiting reagent
3Do every further calculation from the limiting reagent only
Excess left
n(given) − n(used)
Used = coefficient × smallest quotient
% yield (standard definition)
actual / theoretical × 100
Listed in the section title; the notes develop only the limiting reagent
Memory hooks
  • Divide by the coefficient, then pick the minimum. Never compare raw moles.
Traps and exceptions
  • Ratio equals coefficients means no excess. 3 mol Na2CO3 + 6 mol HCl: nothing is left, CO2 = 3 mol = 67.2 L.
  • Convert grams to moles first. The larger mass is not always the excess reagent.
Worked example: 6 mol Na2CO3 + 4 mol HCl (Na2CO3 + 2HCl → 2NaCl + CO2 + H2O)

6/1 = 6 and 4/2 = 2. The minimum is 2, so HCl is limiting. n(CO2) = 2 mol, V = 2 × 22.4 = 44.8 L.

5 · Principle of atom conservation (POAC)

The big idea. Atoms are conserved in any chemical reaction (not in nuclear ones), so moles of an atom are the same before and after. POAC is the shortcut when the equation is unknown or there are many steps: follow just one atom.
Moles of an atom
atoms per formula unit × moles of compound
1 mol K2CO3 holds 1 mol C
General POAC
p · wA/MA = q · wB/MB
p, q = atoms of the chosen element per formula unit of A and B
KClO3 → KCl + O2
K: n(KClO3) = n(KCl)
O: 3 n(KClO3) = 2 n(O2) = 2 V/22.4
Memory hooks
  • Pick one atom, count it on both sides. Ignore the intermediate steps entirely.
Traps and exceptions
  • Count atoms per formula unit carefully. K2Zn3[Fe(CN)6]2 has 2 × 6 = 12 C.
  • Impure sample (standard idea): take the pure mass (purity % × sample mass) first, then apply POAC.
Worked example: 27.6 g K2CO3 (138) converted fully to K2Zn3[Fe(CN)6]2 (698)

Conserve C: 27.6/138 × 1 = w/698 × 12. So w = 0.2 × 698/12 = 11.6 g.

6 · Oxidation number

The big idea. Oxidation number is an imaginary charge: bonding electrons are assumed to go completely to the more electronegative atom. It is a bookkeeping tool built on a fixed set of rules.

Rules

SpeciesOxidation numberExceptions
F−1 always—
O−2Peroxide −1 (H2O2, Na2O2); superoxide −1/2 (KO2); ozonide −1/3 (KO3); OF2 +2; O2F2 +1
H+1Metal hydrides −1 (NaH, KH)
Cl, Br, I−1Positive with a more electronegative atom (KClO3 +5, HClO4 +7)
Alkali / alkaline earth / Al+1 / +2 / +3Other metals can be zero or negative
Free element or allotrope0O2, S8, P4, O3
Sum0 in a molecule; = charge in an ion—
p-block range
(n − 10) to (n − 18)
n = group number; N (group 15): −3 to +5
Maximum oxidation number
≤ valence electrons
Mainly for p-block elements
Memory hooks
  • Fix first, solve last: set F, O, H and the metals, then solve for the unknown from the total.
Traps and exceptions
  • Average, not individual. S is +2 in Na2S2O3 and +2.5 in Na2S4O6, but these are averages over the S atoms.
  • O is −1 in H2O2 but −2 in H2O.
  • Hydrogen can be −1 in NaH.
Worked example: S in Na2S4O6

(+1) × 2 + 4x + (−2) × 6 = 0, so 4x = 10 and x = +2.5.

7 · Redox, agents & disproportionation

The big idea. Oxidation and reduction always happen together. In every redox reaction the total increase in oxidation number equals the total decrease.
OxidationReduction
Addition of oxygen (2Mg + O2 → 2MgO)Removal of oxygen (CuO + C → Cu + CO)
Removal of hydrogenAddition of hydrogen
Increase in positive charge (Fe2+ → Fe3+)Decrease in positive charge
Increase in oxidation numberDecrease in oxidation number (MnO4− → Mn2+)
Loss of electronsGain of electrons
Oxidising agent
oxidation number falls / gains e⁻
KMnO4, K2Cr2O7, HNO3, conc. H2SO4
Reducing agent
oxidation number rises / loses e⁻
K, Na2S2O3
Both
H2O2, NO2−
Intermediate oxidation state

Disproportionation

The same element, in an intermediate state, is oxidised and reduced at once. Examples: 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2; Cl2 + 2OH− → ClO− + Cl− + H2O; 4KClO3 → 3KClO4 + KCl; P4 + OH− → PH3 + H2PO2−.

Memory hooks
  • Dis = one element goes both ways (up and down). Com = two states meet in one product.
Traps and exceptions
  • 2KClO3 → 2KCl + 3O2 is not disproportionation: Cl is reduced but O is oxidised, two different elements.
  • NH4NO2 → N2 + 2H2O is comproportionation: N at −3 and +3 meet at 0.
  • F2 never disproportionates because it is the most electronegative element.
  • Changing the medium (acid to base) can run a disproportionation backwards.

8 · Balancing redox (ion–electron method)

The big idea. A balanced equation needs atom balance and charge balance. The ion–electron (half-reaction) method is preferred over the oxidation-number-change method.

Steps (acidic medium)

#Step
1Assign oxidation numbers
2Write the net ionic equation (drop spectators)
3Identify what is oxidised and reduced; split into two half-reactions
4Balance atoms other than O and H
5Balance O with H2O, then H with H+
6Balance charge by adding e− to the positive side
7Multiply to equalise electrons, add the halves
8Restore spectator ions to get the molecular equation
Excess oxygen (acidic)
+ H2O on the other side, + 2H+ on the same side
One H2O and 2H+ per excess O
Basic medium
add OH− = number of H+ to both sides
Then H+ + OH− → H2O
Memory hooks
  • Order matters: atoms (not O, H) → O with H2O → H with H+ → charge with e−.
Traps and exceptions
  • Electrons go on the positive side. MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O.
  • Check both balances at the end. Atoms and net charge must match.
Worked example (acidic): Fe2+ with MnO4−

Fe2+ → Fe3+ + e− (× 5); MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O. Sum: 5Fe2+ + MnO4− + 8H+ → 5Fe3+ + Mn2+ + 4H2O. Molecular: 10FeSO4 + 2KMnO4 + 8H2SO4 → 5Fe2(SO4)3 + 2MnSO4 + K2SO4 + 8H2O.

Worked example (basic): ClO− with CrO2−

2e− + H2O + ClO− → Cl− + 2OH− (× 3); 4OH− + CrO2− → CrO42− + 2H2O + 3e− (× 2). Sum: 3ClO− + 2CrO2− + 2OH− → 3Cl− + 2CrO42− + H2O.

9 · Concentration, dilution & mixing

The big idea. A solution is a homogeneous mixture. Concentration terms are all linked, so one of them lets you find the rest. Only molarity changes with temperature (volume changes); molality and mole fraction do not.
Molarity (M)
mol solute / L solution = w × 1000 / (M0 × VmL)
Millimoles = M × V(mL). Falls as T rises
Molality (m)
mol solute / kg solvent
Independent of temperature
Mole fraction
x1 = n/(n + N), x1 + x2 = 1
Pure number; independent of T
% w/w · % w/v · % v/v
solute / solution × 100
per 100 g · per 100 mL · per 100 mL
ppm (by mass)
mass of A / total mass × 106
100 ppm = 100 g in 106 g
Dilution / mixing
M1V1 = M2V2 · MR = (M1V1 + M2V2)/(V1 + V2)
Same solute only
Memory hooks
  • Molarity = per litre of solution; molality = per kg of solvent. M for Mixture of volumes, m for mass.
Traps and exceptions
  • Mixing acids with different ions: 200 mL 1 M HCl + 100 mL 0.5 M H2SO4 gives [H+] = (200 + 100 × 0.5 × 2)/300 = 1 M.
  • % w/w uses the mass of the whole solution in the denominator (0.5 g in 25 g solvent: 0.5/25.5 = 1.96 %).
  • Source typo: the urea example says 225 g of solution but subtracts from 255. With 225 g, the solvent is 220 g and m ≈ 0.38; the PDF's 0.332 uses 250 g.
Worked example: 149 g KCl (74.5) in 10 L of solution

149/74.5 = 2 mol; M = 2/10 = 0.2 M.
