Group 1 — Napier's Analogy G1ALLEN Pt 05, 13(c) · Ex3 Q5 · Ex4 Q14 · Ex5 Q1 · PT2 Q6, Q11

1\(\tan\dfrac{A-B}{2}=\dfrac{a-b}{a+b}\cot\dfrac C2\) (and cyclic). Trigger: two sides + included angle with awkward numbers, or \(A-B\) / \(B-C\) given.
2Procedure: \(A+B=180^\circ-C\) known → Napier gives \(A-B\) → solve the two linear equations → third side by sine or cosine rule.
3\(\cos(A-B)\) given: \(\cos(A-B)=\dfrac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}\), \(t=\tan\tfrac{A-B}2\) → \(\cot\tfrac C2\) (Ex5 Q1: \(t=\tfrac13\cot\tfrac C2\Rightarrow\cot\tfrac C2=1\Rightarrow C=90^\circ,\Delta=9\)).
4Check: the angle must be the included one; keep the half in \(\tfrac{A-B}2\); after \(\cos C\) use the cosine rule for \(c\).
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. What is given? Two sides and the included angle, or the difference \(A-B\) / \(B-C\) together with sides.
  2. What is asked? The other angles, an angle ratio, the area or the third side. If the numbers are awkward (surds), Napier beats the cosine rule.
  3. Write the angle sum first: \(A+B=180^\circ-C\). That is already half of the answer.
  4. Pair correctly: the two given sides give \(\tfrac{a-b}{a+b}\); the angle between them gives \(\cot\tfrac C2\).
  5. Get \(\tfrac{A-B}2\), double it, solve with \(A+B\), then verify with \(\tfrac a{\sin A}=\tfrac b{\sin B}\).
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • SAS solving: Napier gives the angles; then the third side by the sine rule (or \(c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\) when \(C=90^\circ\)).
  • \(\cos(A-B)\) given: \(\tan^2\tfrac{A-B}2=\tfrac{1-\cos(A-B)}{1+\cos(A-B)}\). Ex3 Q5: \(\tfrac{1/32}{63/32}=\tfrac1{63}\), then \(\tan\tfrac{A-B}2=\tfrac{a-b}{a+b}\cot\tfrac C2\) gives \(\cot\tfrac C2\), \(\cos C=\tfrac18\), \(c=6\).
  • \(A-B\) found from sides: Ex4 Q14: \(\tan\tfrac{A-B}2=\tfrac{2}{2\sqrt3}\cot30^\circ=1\Rightarrow A-B=90^\circ\); with \(A+B=120^\circ\): \(105^\circ,15^\circ\), ratio 7:1.
  • Forgot the formula? Derive it: \(\tfrac{a-b}{a+b}=\tfrac{\sin A-\sin B}{\sin A+\sin B}=\tfrac{\tan\frac{A-B}2}{\tan\frac{A+B}2}\) and \(\tan\tfrac{A+B}2=\cot\tfrac C2\).
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Hook: difference over sum, tan of half the difference, cot of half the OTHER angle.
  • Sign check: \(a>b\iff A>B\), so the result must be positive when \(a>b\).
  • Recognise special tangents: \(1\to45^\circ\), \(\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\to30^\circ\), \(\sqrt3\to60^\circ\), \(2-\sqrt3\to15^\circ\).
  • Rationalise early: \((\sqrt3+1)(\sqrt3-1)=2\) and \((\sqrt3+1)^2=4+2\sqrt3\).
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Using an angle that is not between the two given sides → check the angle sits between them, or switch to the sine rule.
  • Reading \(\tfrac{A-B}2=45^\circ\) as \(A-B=45^\circ\) → write \"double it\" as its own line.
  • Mixing tan and cot, or using \(C\) instead of \(\tfrac C2\) → write the full formula once and substitute.
  • Treating \(\cos(A-B)\) as \(\cos C\) → they are unrelated; use \(A+B=180^\circ-C\) separately.
  • Stopping at the angles → re-read the last line (third side? area? ratio?).

Group 2 — Ratio of Sums G2Ex2 Q1 · Ex4 Q13, Q21

5If \((b+c):(c+a):(a+b)=p:q:r\), put \(b+c=pk\), \(c+a=qk\), \(a+b=rk\). Add: \(a+b+c=\tfrac{(p+q+r)k}2\); subtract to isolate each side.
611 : 12 : 13 ⇒ \(a+b+c=18k\) ⇒ \(a=7k,\,b=6k,\,c=5k\); \(\cos A:\cos B:\cos C=\tfrac15:\tfrac{19}{35}:\tfrac57=7:19:25\).
77 : 8 : 9 ⇒ \(a+b+c=12k\) ⇒ sides \(4k,3k,5k\): a right triangle, \(R=\tfrac{5k}2,\ r=k\Rightarrow\tfrac Rr=\tfrac52\).
8\(k\) cancels in every angle and every ratio. Spot Pythagorean triples (3-4-5, 5-12-13) before using the cosine rule.
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. The ratio is of sums \((b+c):(c+a):(a+b)\), not of the sides. Never read 11, 12, 13 as sides.
  2. Introduce \(k\): \(b+c=11k,\ c+a=12k,\ a+b=13k\).
  3. Add the three and halve: \(a+b+c=18k\). Then \(a=(a+b+c)-(b+c)\), and similarly for \(b,c\).
  4. Sides: \(7k,6k,5k\). Check the triangle inequality and look for a known triple (3-4-5, 5-12-13).
  5. Only now answer the question (cosines, \(R/r\), angles, area). \(k\) cancels.
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Cosines: \(\cos A=\tfrac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}\) with the \(k\)-sides; write each as a fraction and clear denominators with the LCD (35): \(7:19:25\).
  • Right-triangle shortcut: if \(a^2+b^2=c^2\), \(R=\tfrac c2\) and \(r=\tfrac{a+b-c}2\); for 3:4:5 this gives \(\tfrac Rr=\tfrac52\) (Ex4 Q21).
  • General \(R/r\): \(\tfrac Rr=\tfrac{abc}{4(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}\); \(k^3\) cancels.
  • A ratio of cosines is not a ratio of sides: compute each cosine.
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Take \(k=1\); the proportions do not change.
  • Quick validity check: each given number must be smaller than the sum of the other two (here \(13<11+12\)).
  • Letter matching: \(a\) pairs with \(b+c\) (the side not in the sum).
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Taking 11, 12, 13 as the sides → write the three equations explicitly before solving.
  • Giving \(7k\) to the wrong side → \(a=18k-(b+c)=18k-11k\).
  • Answering with the side ratio when cosines are asked → read the last line of the question.
  • Cosine rule with the wrong opposite side → write \"A is opposite a\" next to each formula.
  • Slips in \(2bc\) → compute numerator and denominator separately, then simplify.

Group 3 — Hidden Progressions G3Ex4 Q7, Q10, Q23 · Ex5 Q2, Q24, Q25 · Ex2 Q5, Q7

9Write \(2b=a+c\) first. \(a,b,c\) in A.P. \(\iff\sin A,\sin B,\sin C\) in A.P. (sine rule). Altitude \(p=\tfrac{2\Delta}{a}\propto\tfrac1a\): altitudes in H.P. \(\iff\) sides in A.P.
10Trig condition → progression: \(\cos A+2\cos B+\cos C=2\Rightarrow2\sin\tfrac B2\cos\tfrac{A-C}2=4\sin^2\tfrac B2\Rightarrow\cos\tfrac{A-C}{2}=2\sin\tfrac B2\Rightarrow a+c=2b\). Then \(b=5\) for sides 3, 7: \(\cos A-\cos C=\tfrac{10}7\).
11A.P. + angle condition: A.P. with \(C=2A\) gives \(4:5:6\). With \(R=1\), \(A-C=\tfrac\pi2\): \(\cos\tfrac{A+C}2=\tfrac{\sqrt2}4\), area \(=\tfrac{3\sqrt7}{16}\) (\((64a)^2=1008\)), \(r=\tfrac14\).
12Angles in A.P. ⇒ \(B=60^\circ\), \(b^2=a^2+c^2-ac\). If \(\tan\tfrac A2,\tan\tfrac B2,\tan\tfrac C2\) are in A.P., convert to cosines using \(\tan\tfrac A2=\tfrac{\Delta}{s(s-a)}\).
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. Scan for A.P./H.P./G.P. — also inside equations (for example \(\cos A+2\cos B+\cos C=2\)).
  2. Write it in sides first: \(2b=a+c\) (H.P.: \(\tfrac2b=\tfrac1a+\tfrac1c\)).
  3. Decide the target (sines? altitudes? exradii?) and use the conversion table below.
  4. Use the second condition (an angle, \(R\), the area) to fix the triangle.
  5. Test the final candidate against every condition, including \"obtuse\", \"largest\" and \"smallest\".
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Conversion table: sides A.P. \(\iff\) sines A.P. \(\iff\) altitudes H.P. \(\iff s-a,s-b,s-c\) A.P. \(\iff r_1,r_2,r_3\) H.P. \(\iff\cot\tfrac A2,\cot\tfrac B2,\cot\tfrac C2\) A.P.
  • \(a^2,b^2,c^2\) A.P. \(\iff\cot A,\cot B,\cot C\) A.P.
  • Hidden A.P. in a trig equation: sum-to-product, then \(\tfrac{a+c}b=\tfrac{\cos\frac{A-C}2}{\sin\frac B2}\) (sine rule and \(\sin\tfrac{A+C}2=\cos\tfrac B2\)). Ex4 Q23 gives \(a+c=2b\), \(b=5\), \(\cos A-\cos C=\tfrac{10}7\).
  • A.P. + angle condition: use \(a=2R\sin A\) (Ex5 Q24/25), or test \(4:5:6\), the A.P. triangle with \(C=2A\).
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Test a claim on 3-4-5: its sides are an A.P., and its altitudes 4, 3, 2.4 are in H.P.
  • Angles in A.P. \(\Rightarrow B=60^\circ\) at once; then \(b^2=a^2+c^2-ac\).
  • Altitude \(p=\tfrac{2\Delta}a\): altitudes behave like the reciprocals of the sides.
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Reversing A.P. and H.P. for altitudes → say it aloud: \"altitude is proportional to 1/side\".
  • Missing the A.P. hidden in a trig equation → apply sum-to-product to every \"cos + cos\" or \"sin + sin\".
  • Assuming 3:4:5 satisfies an extra condition → check it (\(C=2A\) holds for 4:5:6, not 3:4:5).
  • Forgetting that the largest angle of an obtuse triangle is the obtuse one (Ex5 Q24) → name the largest and smallest angles before computing.
  • Stopping after the progression → the question may ask for the area, \((64a)^2\) or \(r\).

Group 4 — R–r Identity Bridge G4Ex3 Q8, Q14 · Ex4 Q22, Q24 · Ex1 Q2, Q16, Q25

13\(r=4R\prod\sin\tfrac A2\), \(\sum\sin A=4\prod\cos\tfrac A2\), \(\sum\sin2A=4\prod\sin A\), \(\sum\cos A=1+\tfrac rR\), \(\sum\cos2A=-1-4\prod\cos A\).
14\(\sum a\cos A=R\sum\sin2A=4R\prod\sin A\); \(\sum a=8R\prod\cos\tfrac A2\) ⇒ \(\dfrac{\sum a\cos A}{\sum a}=4\prod\sin\tfrac A2=\dfrac rR\).
15\(\dfrac r{r_1}=\dfrac{s-a}{s}=\tan\tfrac B2\tan\tfrac C2\) and \(\sum\tan\tfrac A2\tan\tfrac B2=1\) ⇒ \(\tan\tfrac A2(\tan\tfrac B2+\tan\tfrac C2)=1-\tan\tfrac B2\tan\tfrac C2\).
16\(\sum\cos2A\) least ⇒ \(\prod\cos A\) greatest \(=\tfrac18\) ⇒ equilateral: \(a=2\sqrt3\,r\), area \(=3\sqrt3\,r^2\). Conditions on \(x=y=z\): divide the two equations to remove \(t\).
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. Sides, trig and \(R,r,r_i\) mixed together → convert everything to one language: \(R\) and the angles.
  2. Substitute \(a=2R\sin A\) (and \(b,c\)).
  3. Spot sums and products: \(\sum\sin A\), \(\sum\sin2A\), \(\sum\cos A\) → use the \(4\prod\) identities.
  4. For \(r\) or \(r_i\) use half-angle forms: \(r=4R\prod\sin\tfrac A2\), \(\tfrac r{r_1}=\tfrac{s-a}s\).
  5. \"Least\" or \"greatest\"? Find the equality case (equilateral) and check each option there.
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Ratio to \(r/R\): numerator \(\sum a\cos A=R\sum\sin2A=4R\prod\sin A\); denominator \(\sum a=2R\sum\sin A=8R\prod\cos\tfrac A2\); ratio \(=4\prod\sin\tfrac A2=\tfrac rR\).
  • Half-angle products: \(\tan\tfrac B2\tan\tfrac C2=\tfrac{s-a}s=\tfrac r{r_1}\) and \(\sum\tan\tfrac A2\tan\tfrac B2=1\).
  • Removing a parameter (line \(x=y=z=t\)): divide the two equations so \(t\) cancels, then apply the identities (Ex4 Q24: \(\prod\sin\tfrac A2=\tfrac1{16}\)).
  • Extremum: \(\prod\cos A\le\tfrac18\), \(\prod\sin\tfrac A2\le\tfrac18\), \(r\le\tfrac R2\); equality only for the equilateral triangle.
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Equilateral check (side 1): \(R=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\), \(r=\tfrac1{2\sqrt3}\), all angles \(60^\circ\). Test any identity you doubt.
  • 3-4-5 check: \(R=2.5\), \(r=1\), \(\Delta=6\).
  • Mnemonic: sum of sines = product of cosines of halves; sum of \(\sin2A\) = product of sines.
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Swapping sin and cos in \(\sum\sin A=4\prod\cos\tfrac A2\) → verify with the equilateral triangle.
  • Dropping the 4 or 8 → remember \(\prod\sin A=8\prod\sin\tfrac A2\prod\cos\tfrac A2\).
  • Leaving \(a\) and \(\sin A\) mixed → convert fully before simplifying.
  • Inverting \(r/R\) → recall \(r\le\tfrac R2\), so \(\tfrac rR\le\tfrac12\).
  • Using the equality case but not checking every option → compute each option at the equilateral triangle (Ex4 Q22).

Group 5 — Translating a Setting G5Ex4 Q3, Q16, Q18 · Ex5 Q20 · Ex1 Q22, Q23

17Medians: \(AG=\tfrac23AD\); solve the small triangle \(ABG\) (angles \(30^\circ,60^\circ\Rightarrow\angle AGB=90^\circ\)); \([ABC]=3[ABG]\).
18Coordinates + equilateral: distance from the centroid to a side's line \(=r\), and \(R=2r\), so \(R+r=3r\).
19Reciprocal triangle: the smallest side \(a\) gives the largest reciprocal, so \(\tfrac1{a^2}=\tfrac1{b^2}+\tfrac1{c^2}\); with \(a=c\sin\theta,b=c\cos\theta\): \(s^4-3s^2+1=0\), \(s=\tfrac{\sqrt5-1}2\).
20Habit: write one sentence \"given ⇒ relation\" before calculating, and finish with a numerical sanity check.
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. Draw the figure and mark every given length and angle.
  2. Translate each phrase into a triangle fact: median → centroid 2:1; line + centroid → distance \(=r\); reciprocals form a right triangle → new Pythagoras; vectors → \(|MA||MB|\cos\angle AMB\); 3-D line \(x=y=z\) → put \(x=y=z=t\).
  3. Write one sentence: \"given ⇒ relation\". Then compute.
  4. Sanity-check numerically (areas positive, sides ordered, a special case).
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Centroid: \(AG=\tfrac23AD\); the three triangles around \(G\) have equal area, so \([ABC]=3[ABG]\).
  • Equilateral with centroid at the origin: \(r=\tfrac{|c|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}\) (distance to the line), \(R=2r\), so \(R+r=3r\).
  • Reciprocal right triangle: the smallest side gives the largest reciprocal, so it is the hypotenuse. Solve the biquadratic with \(u=\sin^2\theta\) and reject \(u>1\).
  • Vectors at the incentre: \(\angle AMB=90^\circ+\tfrac C2\); in an equilateral triangle \(120^\circ\) and \(MA=MB=2r\), so the dot product is \(-\tfrac12(2r)^2=-18\) for \(r=3\).
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Test a derived relation on a special case (equilateral, 3-4-5).
  • In an MCQ, plug each option back into the setting.
  • Name every symbol: \"\(r\)\" means the distance from the centre to a side.
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Treating the given median length as \(AG\) → \(AG\) is two-thirds of it.
  • Forgetting the factor 3 in the area → \([ABG]\) is only one third.
  • Confusing \(R\) and \(r\) → the distance from the centre to a side is \(r\).
  • Using the wrong angle for vectors → take the angle between the vectors, \(\angle AMB\), not an angle of the triangle.
  • Keeping an impossible root (\(\sin^2\theta>1\)) → check each root against the setting.

Group 6 — Algebra Reshaping & Existence G6Ex3 Q2, Q3 · Ex4 Q15 · Ex5 Q8, Q14

21\(2a^2b^2+2b^2c^2=a^4+b^4+c^4\): add \(2a^2c^2\) ⇒ \((a^2+c^2-b^2)^2=2a^2c^2\) ⇒ \(\cos^2B=\tfrac12\); acute ⇒ \(B=\tfrac\pi4\).
22Area + perimeter: \(\tfrac12ab\sin C=\Delta\Rightarrow ab\); \(c^2=(a+b)^2-2ab(1+\cos C)\) with \(a+b=P-c\).
23Existence: apply all three triangle inequalities. Sides \(5,5r,5r^2\): \(r^2-r-1<0\) and \(r^2+r-1>0\) ⇒ \(0.62<r<1.62\); \(r=\tfrac74\) fails.
24Test the options when expansion is heavy (Ex5 Q14: \(x=1+\sqrt3\) makes \(b=c\), \(\cos C=\tfrac a{2b}=0.866\)). \(a=2R\sin A\) makes \((a,\sin A,R)\) redundant.
🧠 How to think — step by step
  1. Symmetric in \(a^2,b^2,c^2\)? Try to make \((a^2+c^2-b^2)^2\) appear, i.e. \(4a^2c^2\cos^2B\).
  2. Area, perimeter and an angle together? Use \(\tfrac12ab\sin C\), the cosine rule and \(c^2=(a+b)^2-2ab(1+\cos C)\).
  3. \"Cannot be\" or \"possible\"? Apply the triangle inequality to all three pairs.
  4. A parameter \(x\) inside the sides? Test the options with the cosine rule instead of expanding.
🛠 Methods & techniques
  • Ex3 Q2: add \(2a^2c^2\) to \(a^4+b^4+c^4=2a^2b^2+2b^2c^2\): \((a^2+c^2-b^2)^2=2a^2c^2\Rightarrow\cos^2B=\tfrac12\); acute \(\Rightarrow\tfrac\pi4\).
  • Ex3 Q3: \(ab=40\) from the area; \(c^2=(a+b)^2-3ab=(20-c)^2-120\Rightarrow c=7\).
  • Ex4 Q15: \(5+5r>5r^2\) and \(5r+5r^2>5\) give \(0.62<r<1.62\); \(r=\tfrac74\) fails.
  • Data sufficiency (Ex5 Q8): the data must carry three independent facts; \(a=2R\sin A\) makes \((a,\sin A,R)\) redundant.
💡 Tips & tricks
  • Degenerate warning: if your manipulation gives \(\cos^2B=1\), the triangle is degenerate — you added or dropped a term. It must come out strictly between 0 and 1.
  • Quadratic inequalities: sketch the parabola; the roots here are \(\tfrac{1\pm\sqrt5}2\).
  • Decimal-check each option (Ex5 Q14: \(x=1+\sqrt3\) gives \(b=c\), \(\cos C=\tfrac a{2b}=0.866\)).
⚠ Common mistakes — and how to avoid them
  • Copying a misprinted equation without testing it → try it on an equilateral triangle first (the book's Ex3 Q2 has a typo).
  • Checking only one triangle inequality → write all three.
  • Using \(c^2=(a+b)^2-2ab\) with no \(\cos C\) → for \(C=60^\circ\) the term \(2ab\cos C\) equals \(ab\).
  • Accepting a root that makes a side negative (Ex5 Q14) → check every side \(>0\).
  • Ignoring \"acute\" or \"obtuse\" → it chooses between \(\tfrac\pi4\) and \(\tfrac{3\pi}4\).
